# War and Armed Conflict Survival Guide

Surviving active armed conflict requires fast decisions, constant situational awareness, and the discipline to stay calm when everything around you is chaos. This guide covers the most urgent scenarios civilians face during war, shelling, airstrikes, and civil breakdown.

---

## Part 1: Bombs and Airstrikes

### When You Hear an Incoming Bomb or Artillery Shell

You often have 3–10 seconds of warning from the sound of an incoming shell before it hits. Sound travels slower than the shell — if you hear a whistle or shriek, the round is already very close.

**Immediate action — indoors:**
1. Drop immediately — do not run, do not stand up
2. Move away from windows, glass, and exterior walls — these become lethal shrapnel in a blast
3. Get into an interior room (hallway, stairwell, bathroom) — ideally below ground level
4. Crouch or lie flat behind a heavy concrete wall, stone column, or furniture if no interior room is accessible
5. Open your mouth slightly — this equalizes ear pressure and reduces the chance of burst eardrums from the overpressure wave
6. Cover your head with your arms and tuck your face toward your chest

**Immediate action — outdoors:**
1. Do not run — you cannot outrun a blast radius, and moving makes you a larger target
2. Drop flat on the ground, face down, feet toward the expected impact direction — the blast wave travels outward from the point of impact
3. Find a ditch, depression, rubble pile, earthen berm, or any terrain feature below ground level — even a few inches of earth cover dramatically reduces fragmentation injury
4. Stay flat until at least 5 seconds after you feel the last shockwave — secondary explosions and shell clusters are common
5. After the blast, do not stand upright immediately — crawl to the nearest hard cover before assessing your situation

**What the blast actually does to you:**
- The overpressure wave causes the most damage to air-filled organs: lungs, eardrums, sinuses, and bowels. Being behind solid cover and lying flat with your mouth slightly open reduces this risk
- Fragmentation (shrapnel) from the casing and debris is the primary cause of casualties — get below ground level or behind solid mass, not just concealment
- The fireball and heat are dangerous only within the immediate vicinity of the crater

### If a Bomb Is Falling Toward Your Home

**If you have any warning (air raid sirens, news alerts, sound of approaching aircraft):**
1. Move immediately to your building's basement or underground shelter — this is the single most important action
2. If no basement exists: go to the lowest floor, an interior room with no windows, and get under a heavy table or in a bathtub (porcelain and the surrounding walls provide some protection from debris)
3. Bring water, documents, phone (charged), a first aid kit, and warm clothes — power will likely fail
4. Stay away from load-bearing walls that face the street — these fail first when a structure is struck
5. If you must stay in an apartment building: middle floors in the center of the building are safer than top floors (which collapse) or ground floor windows
6. Do not shelter in underground parking garages with fuel — these become firebombs if struck

**After a strike on your building:**
- Do not use elevators — electrical systems fail and you can be trapped
- Before opening interior doors, feel the door with the back of your hand — if hot, do not open (fire on the other side)
- If the building is structurally compromised (you hear cracking, feel the floor shift, see cracks spreading), exit immediately using the stairwell, staying close to the inner wall
- If you are trapped under rubble: do not shout continuously — it exhausts air and your voice. Shout in three-burst intervals, then listen. Tap on pipes or concrete with a hard object — this sound travels farther than voice

### Open Space During an Airstrike or Shelling

Being caught in the open during active bombardment is the most dangerous situation. Your goal is to minimize your profile and reach cover in the shortest time.

**If caught in the open:**
1. Do not run in a straight line — zigzag movement toward cover reduces predictability for targeted fire
2. The nearest ditch, crater, or depression is your primary refuge — paradoxically, a fresh shell crater is relatively safe because shells rarely land in the same spot twice, and the crater provides immediate below-ground cover
3. If there is no terrain feature: lie flat in the lowest point you can reach, arms over your head, mouth open, feet toward the blast direction
4. A metal vehicle provides concealment but not meaningful cover against artillery — the engine block is the only section that stops rounds
5. Concrete walls, earth embankments, stone structures, and masonry walls provide actual cover — get behind the thickest, densest mass you can reach

**Blast radius reference:**
- A mortar round has a lethal fragmentation radius of roughly 50m and a wounding radius of 100m
- Artillery rounds: 200–400m lethal, 600m+ wounding from a heavy shell
- A large airstrike bomb can be lethal to 300m and damaging to 700m+ depending on munition type
- If you can hear a whistle but no explosion: the round passed overhead and landed elsewhere — stay low and wait

## Part 2: Active Gunfire and Shooting

### When Shooting Starts Near You

**First two seconds are critical — your brain will try to freeze. Override it:**
1. Drop to the ground or take immediate cover without hesitation
2. Identify the direction of fire — watch for muzzle flash, dust kick-up near the ground, where people are running from, or the direction of sound
3. Move away from the fire — do not move toward it, do not stand and look
4. Do not shout — it reveals your position
5. If you are with others, signal with hand gestures and keep moving

**Understanding cover vs. concealment — this distinction kills people:**
- **True cover** (stops bullets): reinforced concrete walls and pillars, engine blocks of vehicles, sandbags, earthen berms 18+ inches thick, stone masonry walls, cast iron manhole covers
- **Concealment** (hides you but does NOT stop bullets): wooden doors, drywall, car doors and body panels, bushes, plastic barriers, shadows
- Many people die sheltering behind concealment they mistake for cover. Glass from windows, plywood, thin walls, and vegetation do not stop bullets

**Movement under fire:**
1. Move in short, fast bursts of 3–5 seconds at a time — this is harder to track and engage than continuous movement
2. Always know where your next cover position is before you leave your current one
3. Keep low — a crouched sprint or crawl presents a much smaller target
4. Change direction unpredictably during movement
5. If you must cross an open area: go fast, go low, go directly to the next solid cover
6. Do not expose the same position twice — if you looked around a corner once, do not look around the same corner again from the same spot

### Sheltering During Sustained Gunfire Nearby

1. Find an interior room away from exterior walls — bullets penetrate exterior walls, especially in residential construction
2. Lie flat on the floor — most rounds travel horizontally, so floor level is the safest position
3. Stay away from doors and windows — even closed windows do not stop bullets
4. Do not use mobile phones or lights that could draw attention if gunfire is in your immediate area
5. Barricade the door with heavy furniture to buy time — not to stop bullets, but to slow entry
6. If you hear a door being forced, move to the farthest corner from it, behind the heaviest object available, and stay low and silent

### Sniper Fire

Sniper fire is harder to detect because there is no visible muzzle flash at distance and the shot arrives before the sound.

- Move unpredictably between cover positions — do not walk in straight lines in the open
- Do not silhouette yourself against the sky, light sources, or open windows
- Cross open areas at ground level or in a crouch rather than upright
- If you hear an unexpected impact near you with no obvious source: take cover immediately and move, do not stand and look
- Avoid reflective surfaces, windows, and bright clothing that create visibility at distance

## Part 3: When Communications Are Down

### Network Outage in a Conflict Zone

When mobile networks fail — due to infrastructure destruction, government shutdown, or congestion — your digital lifelines disappear. Plan for this before it happens.

**When network dies:**
1. Save your phone battery — turn off WiFi, mobile data, Bluetooth. Go to airplane mode and only disable it for brief attempts to connect
2. Try calling over WiFi if local routers still work — WhatsApp, Signal, and Telegram work over WiFi even without cellular
3. SMS sometimes gets through when voice calls fail — texts use less bandwidth and can queue and transmit during brief network availability
4. If total blackout: pre-designated meeting points with family become critical — choose one near home and one farther away before a crisis

**Alternative communication methods:**
- **FM radio**: Battery-powered or hand-crank radios receive emergency broadcasts without any network. Many countries maintain emergency FM frequencies during war
- **Shortwave radio**: Can receive international broadcasts (BBC World Service, Radio Free Europe) from outside the conflict zone — provides news and evacuation information
- **Physical messages**: Leave written notes in pre-agreed locations with trusted neighbors or family members
- **Runners**: In tight communities, trusted people can carry messages on foot between locations when electronic communication fails

**Offline navigation without data:**
- Download offline maps of your city before a crisis (Google Maps, Maps.me, OsmAnd all support offline download) — these work with GPS without any data connection
- GPS satellites continue to function even when communications are down — your phone's GPS chip works independently of cellular networks
- Paper maps are invaluable — keep one with your emergency documents
- Know cardinal directions from your home: identify which way is north in relation to your street and the nearest safe zone

**When to move vs. when to stay:**
- **Stay** if: your shelter is structurally sound, you have food and water for 2+ weeks, roads are actively under fire or blocked, you have elderly or injured people who cannot travel
- **Move** if: your building is no longer structurally safe, you have no water or medical supplies, you have a clear escape route, and there is a specific safe destination to reach
- Never evacuate in the middle of active fighting unless your life is in immediate danger inside your shelter — moving through active fire is more dangerous than sheltering

## Part 4: Evacuation Under Fire

### Planning Your Escape Route

1. Know three different routes out of your neighborhood — if one road is blocked by rubble or checkpoints, you need alternatives
2. Identify safe waypoints: buildings with basements, hospitals, schools, places of worship
3. Know the location of the nearest official evacuation corridor — governments and international organizations often designate specific routes and times
4. Identify hazards on your routes: bridges (can be destroyed), open plazas (provide no cover), known checkpoint locations

### The Evacuation Kit (Go-Bag)

Keep this packed and accessible at all times during a crisis:
- **Documents**: passport, ID, birth certificates — in a waterproof bag. Photograph everything and store in cloud email drafts
- **Cash in small denominations**: ATMs and card systems fail during conflict. Small bills are essential
- **Water**: minimum 3 liters per person for 24–48 hours of movement
- **Food**: high-calorie, compact, non-perishable — energy bars, nuts, dried fruit, canned protein
- **First aid**: tourniquet, hemostatic gauze, bandages, antiseptic, prescription medications (30+ day supply), pain relievers
- **Warmth**: emergency space blankets, one change of clothes, rain layer
- **Light**: headlamp with spare batteries — hands-free is essential
- **Phone charger + power bank**: charged to 100%
- **Whistle**: for signaling if trapped

### Moving Through a Conflict Zone

**By vehicle:**
- Drive with interior lights on when approaching checkpoints
- Do not drive at speed toward any checkpoint — you may be shot as a threat
- If taking fire while driving: accelerate out of the kill zone rather than stopping
- Park away from potential targets: military vehicles, government buildings, communication towers
- Mark your vehicle with large white strips of cloth on the roof and hood — signals civilian status to aircraft

**On foot:**
- Move during daylight along pre-planned routes
- Stay close to walls and buildings, not in the middle of streets
- Do not carry anything that could be mistaken for a weapon
- White cloth held above your head signals civilian status when approaching military positions
- Do not run near military personnel or checkpoints — slow, visible movements with hands raised
- Travel in small groups (3–6 people), not large crowds

### Checkpoint Crossings

1. Slow down 100+ meters before the checkpoint — be visible before you arrive
2. Turn on interior lights at night
3. Follow all instructions immediately and without argument
4. Keep hands visible at all times — do not reach into bags or pockets without being explicitly told to
5. Do not make jokes, sudden movements, or aggressive eye contact
6. If traveling with women or children, ensure they are visible
7. Do not carry weapons or military equipment — this is an immediate shooting risk
8. If asked for documents, move slowly and clearly explain what you are reaching for
9. If detained: stay calm, do not resist, state that you are a civilian, state your destination and origin

## Part 5: Shelter-in-Place During Sustained Bombardment

### Selecting the Best Shelter in a Building

**Best option — basement or underground floor:**
- Multiple layers of reinforced concrete above you dramatically reduce the chance of a direct strike penetrating to your level
- Stay away from windows and light wells — blast waves travel through openings
- Move toward the center of the basement — exterior basement walls can be breached

**Second option — interior rooms on lower floors:**
- Bathrooms are often strong rooms: plumbing walls contain extra supports, and the bathtub provides a low-profile protected position
- Interior stairwells in concrete buildings are often the strongest structural element
- Corridors without windows are good options if lined by structural walls

**Worst positions during bombardment:**
- Any room with windows facing the street or direction of fire
- Top floors — most likely to receive direct hits, and upper floor collapses cascade down
- Glass-walled rooms
- Near fuel sources: kitchens with gas lines, parking garages

### Water and Food During Extended Shelter-in-Place

**Water:**
- Fill every available container (bathtubs, pots, bottles, buckets) at the first sign of a crisis — water infrastructure is often targeted early
- A bathtub holds 150–300 liters — enough for weeks of drinking
- Toilet tanks (not bowls) hold clean water. Water heaters hold 100–200 liters
- Purification without utilities: boiling is most reliable; iodine or chlorine tablets as backup; UV purification using clear plastic bottles in direct sunlight for 6+ hours as last resort

**Food:**
- Prioritize non-perishable, high-calorie foods: canned goods, dried legumes, rice, pasta, nuts, cooking oil
- Maintain food discipline — ration from day one if resupply is uncertain

### Mental Health During Extended Shelter

- Maintain a daily routine: fixed times for waking, eating, sleeping — structure prevents the spiral of helplessness
- Limit news consumption to specific times — constant exposure paralyzes decision-making
- Assign tasks to each person in the shelter — purposeful work reduces panic
- Allow children to express fear and anxiety — give them age-appropriate tasks and reassurance
- Helping others is the most effective short-term buffer against psychological collapse
- Keep a written journal — it externalizes stress and maintains a clear timeline of events

## Part 6: Blast Injury Treatment

### Recognizing Blast Injuries

Blast injuries are complex and often not immediately apparent.

**Primary blast injury** (from overpressure wave):
- Symptoms: ringing in ears, disorientation, bloody cough, abdominal pain — these indicate internal damage to air-filled organs
- Treatment: lie flat, keep still, do not give food or water (surgery may be required), monitor breathing closely
- Bloody cough after a blast is a medical emergency — lung injury can worsen rapidly over hours

**Secondary blast injury** (fragmentation):
- Treat like penetrating wounds: for major bleeding, apply tourniquet or wound packing
- Multiple small fragment wounds may not bleed dramatically but can cause life-threatening internal damage — monitor vital signs even if wounds appear minor

**Tertiary blast injury** (body thrown by blast):
- Do not move a person who was thrown significant distance unless in immediate danger — spinal injury is likely
- Treat as trauma: check for fractures and internal organ injury

**Blast concussion:**
- Symptoms: confusion, amnesia, headache, altered behavior, brief loss of consciousness
- Keep the person still, cool, and monitored — do not leave alone
- Someone who has experienced multiple nearby blasts may seem fine but decline over hours

## Part 7: IED Awareness

### Recognizing IED Indicators

In areas of recent fighting, treat all unfamiliar objects as potential threats:
- Disturbed earth, fresh patches of asphalt, or unusual road markings
- Wires or cords running from the road into bushes, walls, or buildings
- Abandoned vehicles parked in unusual positions (blocking a road, parked where vehicles normally don't stop)
- Unusual objects on roadsides: bags, boxes, canisters, piles of debris that seem deliberately placed
- Pressure plates: thin boards or tiles that look slightly raised or differently colored from surroundings

**If you suspect an IED:**
1. Stop immediately — do not step forward
2. Do not use a mobile phone within 50 meters — radio signals can trigger some devices
3. Warn others to stay back — 300+ meters
4. Retrace your exact steps — step only where you have already stepped
5. Report to authorities or military if possible

## Part 8: Psychological Survival

### Managing Fear Under Fire

Fear is a physiological response — your body floods with adrenaline to prepare for action. Extreme fear degrades decision-making. These techniques help:

- **Controlled breathing**: Breathe in for 4 counts, hold for 4, out for 4. This activates the parasympathetic system and slows the stress response within 30–60 seconds. Do this before moving, before making decisions
- **Narrow your focus**: Do not think about the overall situation — think only about the next single action. "Get behind that wall." Not "survive this entire war."
- **Physical action reduces panic**: Moving, even small movements, breaks the freeze response. Grip something, crawl, load a bag — action restores agency
- **Tunnel vision**: Extreme stress causes loss of peripheral awareness. Consciously scan left and right and make yourself look around your full environment

### After Witnessing Trauma

- Numbness and mechanical behavior in the hours and days after trauma is normal — this is dissociation, a psychological protection mechanism
- Intrusive replays (flashbacks, vivid memories triggered by sounds or smells) are expected — they are not weakness
- Talk to others who shared the experience — isolation amplifies trauma
- Helping others — caring for the injured, organizing supplies — is the most effective short-term buffer against psychological collapse
- Long-term: if sleep, concentration, and emotional regulation remain severely impaired after weeks, seek professional support when it becomes available

# Shelter, Warmth and Being Stranded (India)

This guide covers keeping a body at a survivable temperature and finding your way when you are cut off — whether that is in a collapsed town, on a stranded highway, in a forest, in the Thar, or on a Himalayan road blocked by a slide.

**Shelter is the second priority after airway and bleeding, ahead of water and far ahead of food.** In Indian conditions, "shelter" usually means shade and airflow, not a roof against snow. Get that ranking right and it changes what you build.

---

## Part 1: What Shelter Is Actually For

Ask: **what is trying to kill me?**

- **Sun and heat (most of India, most of the year):** you need **shade and moving air**, and nothing else. A tarpaulin stretched a foot above your head with open sides is vastly better than an enclosed tent, which becomes an oven.
- **Cold and wind (Himalaya, north Indian winter nights, high altitude):** you need **insulation, a windbreak, and separation from the ground**. Ground conduction steals more heat than air ever will.
- **Rain (monsoon India):** you need a **waterproof roof and to stay dry**. Wet clothing at 15 C causes hypothermia. Wetness kills more than cold does.
- **Insects (everywhere, especially post-flood):** you need a **net or a smoke source**. Malaria and dengue are the delayed killers.
- **People:** you need to be **out of sight** or behind a locked door.

Build for the actual threat. Do not build a cave in Rajasthan or an open lean-to in Spiti.

---

## Part 2: Site Selection

Get this right and the shelter itself hardly matters.

**Avoid:**
- **Dry riverbeds, nalas, gullies, and the bottom of any drainage line.** Flash floods. This is the most common fatal camping error in India.
- **The base of a cliff or a cut slope** — rockfall and landslide
- **Under a lone tall tree** — lightning, and in India, falling branches from silk cotton, eucalyptus, and old peepal
- **Under coconut palms** — a falling coconut is lethal
- **Hilltops and open ridges** — wind and lightning
- **Low hollows in cold areas** — cold air pools there at night
- **Directly beside standing water** — mosquitoes
- **Anthills, termite mounds, and rat burrows** — snakes hunt there

**Prefer:**
- **Slightly raised, level, dry ground** with natural drainage
- **On the leeward side of a wall, rock, or dense vegetation** for a windbreak
- **Near but not beside water** — 50-100 m
- **Where you can be seen from the air or a road** if you want rescue
- **Where there is fuel and building material**

## Part 3: Shelter in a Damaged Town or City

- **Do not sleep inside a structurally damaged building.** See the **Earthquake** guide for how to read damage.
- **A vehicle** is a workable shelter: rain-proof, lockable, and elevated. In heat, it is lethal in daytime and fine at night — park in shade, keep windows cracked, never sleep in a running vehicle (carbon monoxide).
- **Under a stairwell, in a doorway of a sound building, or in the lee of a compound wall**
- **A tarpaulin lean-to** against a sound wall: tie two corners high, stake two corners low, and you have a rain and sun shelter in 3 minutes.
- **Schools, temples, mosques, gurudwaras, churches, community halls, and panchayat bhavans** are the standard relief shelters in India and are usually the first to open. Go there rather than improvising.
- **Insulate from concrete.** A concrete floor pulls heat out of you all night. Cardboard, plastic sheet, dry leaves, newspaper, a durrie — anything between you and the slab is worth more than an extra blanket on top.

## Part 4: Improvised Shelters

**Tarpaulin / plastic sheet A-frame (5 minutes)**
Run a rope between two anchors at chest height. Drape the sheet over it. Weight the edges with stones. Done. Add a second sheet on the ground.

**Lean-to (rain and wind from one direction)**
One horizontal pole between two supports at waist-to-chest height, angled poles or branches leaning from it to the ground on the windward side, covered with leaves, thatch, or a sheet, layered from the bottom up like roof tiles so water runs off.

**Debris hut (cold weather, one person, no tools)**
A ridge pole from a low fork to the ground. Ribs leaning against it on both sides. Pile 60 cm or more of leaves, grass, and debris over the whole thing. Stuff the inside with dry leaves and crawl in. **The thickness of the debris is the insulation** — this shelter works down to freezing with no fire.

**Shade shelter (Indian summer — the one you will most often need)**
The critical detail: **two layers with an air gap between them.** A sheet stretched flat, and a second sheet 20-30 cm above it. The air gap carries away the radiant heat and the temperature underneath drops by 10-15 C compared to a single layer. Keep all sides open for airflow. Orient the long axis east-west so the sun tracks along it.

**Desert shelter (Thar, Kutch)**
Dig a shallow trench 30-45 cm deep — the sand a few inches down is dramatically cooler. Cover with a sheet raised above it. Rest during the day, move at night.

**Snow / high altitude**
A trench in the snow with a covered roof, or a hollow dug into a drift. Insulate the floor thickly. **Keep a ventilation hole** and keep it clear — carbon dioxide accumulates in a sealed snow shelter.

## Part 5: Staying Warm

**Layer, and keep the layers dry.**
- **Inner layer:** should wick, not hold water. **Wet cotton against the skin is the single biggest cold-weather mistake in India** — it holds water and conducts heat away. If it is wet, take it off.
- **Middle layer:** traps air. Wool, fleece, a shawl, a blanket, crumpled newspaper stuffed between layers.
- **Outer layer:** blocks wind and water. A plastic sheet, a raincoat, a tarpaulin poncho.

**Get off the ground.** A layer of dry leaves, cardboard, plastic, or a durrie underneath is worth more than two blankets on top.

**Cover the head, neck, hands and feet.** A monkey cap, a muffler, a towel — anything.

**Newspaper is excellent insulation.** Stuff it inside clothing, under a shirt, inside shoes, and between blankets.

**Share body heat.** Two or three people under one set of blankets, with insulation underneath, stay far warmer than three people separately.

**Eat and drink warm things.** The body needs fuel to generate heat. A hot sweet tea genuinely helps.

**Never bring a coal angithi, sigri, charcoal, or a kerosene heater into a closed room to sleep.** Carbon monoxide kills whole families every north Indian winter. If you use one, ventilate on two sides and put it out before sleeping.

## Part 6: Fire

**Uses:** warmth, boiling water (the most important use), cooking, light, keeping animals and insects away, signalling, and morale.

**Building it:**
1. **Tinder** — anything that catches from a spark: dry grass, coconut coir, cotton wool, jute, a bird's nest, dry bark shavings, paper, char cloth. **Coconut coir and dry cow dung are the classic Indian tinders** and both work well.
2. **Kindling** — pencil-thick dry twigs, split bamboo
3. **Fuel** — progressively thicker dead wood, dry dung cakes (uple), bamboo
4. Build a small teepee of kindling over the tinder, light from the upwind side, and add fuel gradually. **Do not smother a young flame.**

**Ignition without matches:**
- **A lighter or matches, kept in a sealed plastic bag** — prepare this, do not rely on improvisation
- **A vehicle battery**: brush the two terminals with a wire or steel wool to spark into tinder
- **A magnifying lens, a spectacle lens, a camera lens, or the bottom of a clear bottle filled with water** focused on tinder in strong Indian sun — this works well and is the most practical improvised method here
- **A ferrocerium rod** — put one in your kit
- **Friction (bow drill)** — works, takes practice, and takes an hour if you have never done it. Do not rely on it.

**Safety:** clear a metre of bare ground around the fire, never leave it unattended, never build under a low canopy or against a dry thatch wall, keep water or sand ready, and **never build a fire inside a closed shelter or a tent**.

**Cooking and boiling:** a steel tiffin box, a bottle cut in half, or even a plastic bottle held just above the flame with water in it (the water stops it melting) will boil water.

## Part 7: If You Are Stranded

**On a highway or in a vehicle:**
- **Stay with the vehicle.** It is shelter, it is visible, and it is what searchers look for. People who walk away from vehicles in the Thar, in Ladakh, and on blocked Himalayan roads are the ones who die.
- Run the engine and heater for 10 minutes an hour in cold, **with the exhaust pipe kept clear of snow or mud** and a window slightly open. Carbon monoxide from a blocked exhaust kills.
- In heat, **get out of the vehicle and shelter in its shade**, not inside it. Use the bonnet or a sheet stretched from the doors.
- Tie something bright to the antenna or the door handle. Raise the bonnet — the universal Indian signal for a broken-down vehicle.
- Ration water; do not ration your decision to seek shade.

**On foot and lost:**
- **STOP: Stop, Think, Observe, Plan.** Sit down for five minutes before doing anything. Most people who die lost, died from moving badly, not from being lost.
- **Stay put if anyone knows where you were going.** Rescue finds stationary people.
- **If you must move: follow water downhill.** In India, watercourses lead to villages. But **do not enter a gorge you cannot climb out of**, and do not follow a river in the Himalaya through a cliff section.
- **Follow power lines, a pipeline, a fence line, or a canal** — all lead to habitation.
- **Move in the cool** — early morning and evening in the plains, midday in the mountains in winter.
- **Mark your route** with stacked stones, broken branches, or cloth so you can retrace and so searchers can follow.

**Navigation without a phone:**
- **The sun rises roughly east and sets roughly west.** At noon in India, shadows point roughly north (you are north of the Tropic of Cancer for most of the country).
- **Shadow-stick method:** put a stick upright in the ground, mark the shadow tip, wait 20 minutes, mark it again. The line between the two marks runs roughly west to east — the first mark is west.
- **At night, find the Pole Star (Dhruv Tara).** Locate the Big Dipper (Saptarishi); the two stars at the end of its bowl point to Polaris. Polaris is due north, and its height above the horizon is roughly your latitude.
- **A leaf floated on water with a magnetised needle** (rubbed on silk or hair) on it points north-south.
- **Download offline maps before you travel** in any hill or remote area. This costs nothing and it is the single best preparation.

## Part 8: Signalling for Rescue

- **Three of anything is the international distress signal** — three whistle blasts, three fires, three shouts, three flashes. Rescuers are trained to recognise it.
- **A whistle carries 3-4 times further than a shout** and costs almost no energy.
- **A mirror, a steel plate, a phone screen, or a piece of foil** flashed at aircraft or a distant road is visible for kilometres in Indian sun. Hold it near your eye, extend the other hand towards the target, and flash across your fingers.
- **Three fires in a triangle**, or one large smoky fire. **Green leaves and grass make white smoke** (visible against dark ground); **rubber, plastic, and oily rags make black smoke** (visible against snow, sand, and sky).
- **Ground signals**, at least 3 metres across, in contrasting colour: **V = need assistance, X = need medical assistance, → = proceeding this way.** Bright saree cloth, stones, or debris.
- **At night, a torch on strobe** is visible far further than a steady beam.
- **Leave a note wherever you have been** — names, number of people, condition, direction of travel, and date and time. This is how search teams narrow the area.

## Part 9: The Psychology of Being Cut Off

Panic and despair kill more survivors than the environment does.

- **Do something.** Any purposeful action — building a shelter, filtering water, laying out a signal — restores a sense of control and measurably improves decision-making. Aimless waiting destroys it.
- **Break the situation into the next single task.** Not "how do we survive a week", but "let's get shade up before noon".
- **Keep a routine.** Fixed times for water, food, signalling, and sleep. Structure is stabilising.
- **Give everybody a job**, including children and the elderly. Being useful is protective.
- **Talk.** Narrate your reasoning out loud if you are alone. It keeps thinking clear and prevents dissociation.
- **Acknowledge fear rather than fighting it.** Fear is normal and fighting the feeling costs energy you need.
- **Ration hope honestly.** Do not promise a rescue time you do not know. Promise the next task instead.

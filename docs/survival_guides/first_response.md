# First Response — The First Five Minutes (India)

This is the entry point for every emergency. If you do not know which guide you need, start here. Everything below assumes phone networks, internet, and possibly electricity have failed, and that no ambulance is coming quickly.

---

## Part 1: The First 60 Seconds

Do these in order. Do not skip ahead.

1. **Stop moving and look up.** Most second injuries in Indian disasters come from falling material — slab concrete, glass, hoardings, electric poles, water tanks. Before you run anywhere, check what is above you.
2. **Get out of the kill zone.** Move away from the immediate hazard: away from the collapsing wall, away from the fire, away from the crowd surge, away from rising water, away from the burning vehicle. Move 30-50 metres minimum if you can.
3. **Check yourself before you check others.** Run your hands over your head, chest, abdomen, groin, and limbs. Look at your hands. Blood you cannot feel is common because adrenaline blocks pain for 10-20 minutes. If you are bleeding heavily, fix that first — a rescuer who bleeds out helps nobody.
4. **Count your people.** Names out loud. Children, elderly, anyone who cannot move themselves. Do this before the dust settles and the crowd scatters.
5. **Decide: stay or move.** You only get one clean decision. Stay if the structure is sound, you have water, and the outside is more dangerous (riot, chemical release, active shooter, cyclone eye passing). Move if there is fire, gas smell, structural cracking, rising water, or the building is leaning.

---

## Part 2: Triage — Who Gets Help First

When several people are hurt and you are alone, you cannot help everyone. Use this order. It feels brutal. It saves the most lives.

**Priority 1 — Treat immediately (minutes to death):**
- Bleeding that is spurting, pooling, or soaking through cloth
- Not breathing, or gasping irregularly
- Airway blocked by blood, vomit, teeth, or the tongue in an unconscious person
- Sucking chest wound (air bubbling from a chest hole)

**Priority 2 — Treat within the hour:**
- Broken bones with no bleeding
- Burns that are not on the face, neck, hands, or genitals
- Head injury but awake and talking
- Abdominal pain, tenderness, rigidity

**Priority 3 — Can wait:**
- Walking, talking, complaining loudly. A person who can shout for help has a clear airway and enough blood pressure to stay conscious. They are stable.

**Priority 4 — Do not spend resources:**
- No pulse, no breathing after a jaw-thrust airway opening, in a mass-casualty situation with no medical help coming

**Practical rule:** Shout "everyone who can walk, come to me." Everyone who walks over is Priority 3. Now you only have to assess the ones who stayed down.

## Part 3: The Order of Survival Needs

Memorise this. It decides where your energy goes.

| Threat | Time to death | Priority |
|---|---|---|
| Blocked airway / no breathing | 3-4 minutes | Immediate |
| Severe arterial bleeding | 3-10 minutes | Immediate |
| Extreme heat (Indian summer, no shade, 45 C+) | 2-6 hours | Very high |
| Extreme cold (Himalayan night, wet) | 3-8 hours | Very high |
| No water | 2-4 days (1-2 days in Indian summer heat) | High |
| No sleep | 4-7 days before severe impairment | Medium |
| No food | 2-3 weeks | Low |

In Indian conditions, **heat and water move up the list**. A person trapped in a collapsed building in Delhi in May will die of hyperthermia and dehydration long before hunger becomes relevant. In the Himalayas in winter, cold moves to the top.

## Part 4: If You Are the Only Adult

You are now the incident commander whether you want to be or not.

- **Give people jobs.** Panic is contagious and idle hands make it worse. "You — hold this cloth on his leg and press hard. You — go find water. You — count how many children we have." Even a shocked person will follow a specific, simple instruction.
- **Speak in short commands, not questions.** "Sit down here." Not "would you like to sit?"
- **Do not announce bad news to the group.** Tell one calm adult privately. Public panic in a crowd is the difference between an evacuation and a stampede.
- **Appoint someone to watch for the hazard.** One person whose only job is to watch the ceiling, or the water level, or the road, and shout if it changes.
- **Write things down.** Names, injuries, time of injury, what you gave them. Use a wall in charcoal if you have no paper. Rescue teams and doctors need this and your memory will fail under stress.

## Part 5: What Is Actually in Your Pocket in India

Assume you have no kit. Here is what an ordinary Indian person is usually carrying or can find within 20 metres.

- **Dupatta, saree pallu, gamcha, lungi, turban cloth** — the single most useful item. Pressure dressing, sling, tourniquet, smoke filter, water pre-filter, rope, stretcher (two cloths and two bamboo poles), signal flag.
- **Phone** — even with no network: torch, clock, camera to document injuries and damage, offline maps if downloaded, and an emergency siren via the ringtone at full volume. Airplane mode makes the battery last 3-5x longer.
- **Steel dabba / tiffin box** — boil water, carry water, dig, bang for signal noise.
- **Bike/scooter** — mirror for heliograph signalling, horn for noise, battery for a light, petrol for fire, seat foam for insulation. Do not siphon petrol by mouth.
- **Auto/car** — headrest posts pull out and their steel prongs break tempered side-window glass. Seat belt webbing is strong cordage. The 12V battery can start a fire with steel wool or foil.
- **Water tank on the roof (Sintex/overhead tank)** — most Indian buildings have 500-1000 litres sitting on the roof. This is drinking water when the supply fails. Do not forget the geyser tank (10-25 litres) and the toilet cistern (not the bowl).
- **Neem, tulsi, turmeric (haldi)** — turmeric powder packed into a wound slows bleeding and has real antibacterial action. Neem leaves boiled make a wound wash. These are supplements to pressure and cleaning, not replacements.

## Part 6: Getting Help When There Is No Network

**112** is India's unified emergency number (Emergency Response Support System, ERSS). It reaches police, fire, ambulance, and disaster response.

Critical facts that people get wrong:
- **112 works with no SIM balance, and on any network your phone can see**, including networks you are not subscribed to. If your operator is down but another operator has a tower nearby, the emergency call can still connect.
- **Voice calls and SMS survive network congestion far better than data.** When WhatsApp will not send, a plain SMS often will. It queues and delivers when a sliver of signal appears.
- **Pressing the power button 3 times fast** on most Android phones triggers the Emergency SOS panic call to 112 with location. Set this up before you need it.
- Older numbers still live in most states: **100** police, **101** fire, **102/108** ambulance, **1077** district disaster control room, **1078** NDMA control room, **1091** women in distress, **1098** child helpline, **1073** road accident.

If nothing connects, see the **Communication Blackout** guide.

## Part 7: The Decision Rules That Kill People

These are the specific errors that appear again and again in Indian disaster post-mortems.

- **Going back inside for documents, jewellery, or a phone charger.** Aadhaar, PAN, and property records can all be re-issued. You cannot. Go back for people only, and only if the structure is sound.
- **Waiting for official confirmation.** In a cyclone, flood, or fire, act on your own eyes. Official warnings in India are good but the last-mile delivery is unreliable, especially when networks are down.
- **Driving into moving water.** 30 cm of moving water floats a hatchback. 60 cm sweeps an SUV. This kills more people in Indian urban floods than drowning in open water.
- **Standing under or near a hoarding, transformer, or tin roof in high wind.** Flying GI sheet is a guillotine.
- **Running toward the exit with everyone else.** In a crowd crush, the exit is the most dangerous place. See the **Crowd and Stampede** guide.
- **Drinking floodwater or open well water without treatment.** Cholera and typhoid follow every Indian flood within 72 hours.
- **Believing a "power is off" claim.** Treat every wire and every metal surface as live until you have personally seen the main isolated.

## Part 8: Building a 72-Hour Readiness Kit (India)

Assemble now, while you have a network and a shop.

**Water and food**
- 3 litres per person per day, minimum 9 litres per person. In summer, double it.
- Chlorine tablets (Aquatabs/halogen tablets) or plain bleach, and a cloth pre-filter
- Dry rations that need no cooking: chana, murmura, sattu, dry fruit, glucose powder, biscuits, jaggery (gur)
- ORS sachets — at least 10 per person. This is the single highest-value item in an Indian kit.

**Medical**
- Cotton roll, crepe bandage, gauze, adhesive tape, Dettol/Savlon, cotton
- Paracetamol, ORS, antacid, antihistamine (cetirizine), antiseptic cream
- Any prescription medicine your household takes — 2 weeks' supply, rotated
- Sanitary pads (also excellent as emergency pressure dressings)

**Power and light**
- Powerbank, fully charged, plus a second one
- Torch with spare cells. A phone torch drains the battery you need for calls.
- Candles and matches in a waterproof bag. Never use a candle where gas may have leaked.

**Documents**
- Photocopies plus photos on the phone plus a copy in DigiLocker: Aadhaar, PAN, ration card, insurance, property papers, bank passbook, prescriptions
- A written list of phone numbers on paper. When your phone dies, your contact list dies with it.
- Some cash in small notes. UPI and cards do not work in a blackout; small vendors cannot give change for 500s.

**Tools**
- Whistle (carries 3-4x further than the human voice and costs no energy)
- Rope, 15 metres
- Multi-tool or a good knife
- N95 masks (dust, smoke, chemical particulate, and post-collapse concrete dust)
- Plastic sheet / tarpaulin
- Battery or hand-crank FM radio — All India Radio keeps broadcasting when everything else is down

**Placement**
- Keep the kit near the exit, not in a loft. Keep footwear next to your bed — most injuries during night evacuations are cut feet from broken glass.

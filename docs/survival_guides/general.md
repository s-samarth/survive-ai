# General Survival Guide

This guide covers foundational survival skills that apply regardless of environment. Master these and you have the building blocks for any situation.

---

## Part 1: The Survival Mindset (S.T.O.P.)

The mind kills before the body. Panic, poor decision-making, and loss of will to live are responsible for more survival deaths than thirst, cold, or predators combined. The first and most important survival tool is your brain.

### S.T.O.P. — The Foundation

When you realize you are in a survival situation, stop and apply this principle before taking any action:

**S — Stop**
Halt all movement immediately. The most dangerous impulse in a survival situation is to move, move, move without a plan. Aimless movement burns calories, consumes water, gets you more lost, and prevents rescue teams from finding you. Stop. Breathe.

**T — Think**
Inventory your situation calmly and systematically:
- Where am I? (Last known location, general direction traveled, time elapsed)
- What are my immediate threats? (Weather, injury, predators, dehydration)
- What resources do I have? (Food, water, equipment, skills, other people)
- What do I need urgently? (In order: air, shelter from extreme temperature, water, fire, food, rescue/navigation)

**O — Observe**
Look around your entire environment:
- What is the weather doing and what direction is it coming from?
- Where is the sun? (Time of day, direction)
- Is there natural shelter, water, or high ground for signaling?
- Are there any immediate dangers you have not yet identified?
- What materials are available for shelter, fire, and tools?

**P — Plan**
Make a deliberate, specific plan before moving. Write it down if you have the means:
- Priority 1: Address immediate life threats (injury treatment, hypothermia, heat stroke)
- Priority 2: Shelter
- Priority 3: Signal for rescue
- Priority 4: Water
- Priority 5: Fire
- Priority 6: Food (the least urgent of the fundamentals — a healthy adult can survive 3 weeks without food)

### The Rule of Threes

Use this to prioritize actions:
- **3 minutes** without air or while in severe shock
- **3 hours** without shelter in extreme weather (can be much faster in cold/wet conditions)
- **3 days** without water
- **3 weeks** without food

This means: fix breathing first, then get out of extreme temperature exposure, then get water, then worry about food.

### Staying Positive

- Panic consumes energy, causes poor decisions, and impairs physical capability. It kills faster than the elements
- Focus on the next single, achievable task — not on the entire survival situation
- Small victories matter: successfully making fire, finding water, building a shelter — these reinforce confidence and maintain will to survive
- If with others: assign roles, keep everyone occupied, and give even the most shocked or frightened person something to do. Purposeful work is psychologically stabilizing
- Talk to yourself if alone — narrating your actions out loud helps maintain clear thinking and prevents dissociation
- Acknowledge that fear is normal — fighting the feeling of fear exhausts energy. Acknowledge it and act anyway

## Part 2: Shelter

Shelter is often the highest survival priority because hypothermia (in cold/wet environments) or hyperthermia (in hot environments) can kill within hours. Never underestimate this.

### Why Shelter Comes Before Water and Food

- A person in wet, cold conditions without shelter can die of hypothermia in 1–3 hours even at temperatures that seem mild (10°C/50°F with rain and wind is deadly without insulation)
- In a desert, unprotected exposure to direct sun can cause heat stroke within 2–4 hours
- You can survive 3+ days without water — shelter buys you time to find it

### Universal Principles of Shelter

**Insulate from the ground:**
- The ground drains body heat far faster than air — conduction into cold or damp ground accounts for 50–60% of heat loss in many outdoor scenarios
- Always create a barrier between your body and the ground: a sleeping pad, a pile of leaves 15cm deep, a layer of pine needles, branches, your backpack, dry clothing — anything is better than direct contact
- Even in a desert where the ground is hot: insulating from the scorching ground prevents burns and conduction of heat into your body

**Minimize exposed surface area:**
- A shelter that fits your body snugly retains heat efficiently — a space that is too large cannot be warmed by body heat alone
- In cold conditions: fill gaps and empty space in your shelter with insulating material (dry leaves, grass, clothing)

**Block wind:**
- Wind-driven convection is one of the most powerful heat-removal mechanisms — even mild wind at cold temperatures causes rapid cooling (wind chill)
- Your shelter's opening should face away from the prevailing wind
- All gaps should be sealed with natural material

**Keep it dry:**
- Wet insulation loses most of its effectiveness — wet clothing can cause hypothermia even at relatively warm temperatures because water conducts heat away from the body 25× faster than air
- A waterproof outer layer on your shelter (bark, leaves layered like shingles, a tarp) is essential in rain

### Debris Hut (Best Primitive Shelter for Cold)

A debris hut can keep you warm even in freezing temperatures with no fire, using only natural materials:

1. **Ridgepole**: find a sturdy pole about 2.5–3m long. Lean one end in the fork of a tree or on a stump about 1m off the ground; the other end on the ground. This is the spine of your shelter
2. **Ribbing**: lean branches of decreasing size diagonally along both sides of the ridgepole, creating a triangular cross-section large enough for your body to enter but no larger. Tighter = warmer
3. **Lattice**: weave sticks horizontally through the diagonal ribs to hold the debris you will pile on top
4. **Pile debris on outside**: heap dry leaves, grass, pine needles, bracken — anything insulating — over the latticework to a depth of at least 60cm (arm's length). This is your insulation. It must be deep and completely covering, including the sides and the top
5. **Door**: stuff a backpack or a pile of leaves and branches into the entrance after getting inside
6. **Inside**: fill the interior with dry leaves to sleep in — this body-contact insulation is as important as the outer debris

This shelter can keep a person alive in subfreezing temperatures through body heat alone.

### Lean-To (Best for Wind and Rain Protection)

A lean-to provides overhead cover and wind protection in a faster build:
1. Lash a horizontal ridgepole between two trees at about chest height
2. Lean branches from the ridgepole to the ground at a 45-degree angle, closely spaced (20–30cm apart)
3. Weave horizontal cross-branches through the diagonal poles
4. Layer leaves or bark from the bottom up, like roof shingles, to a thick depth
5. Build a windbreak wall on the sides if wind is a concern
6. Build your fire at the open front of the lean-to — the angled roof reflects heat back down toward you

## Part 3: Fire

Fire provides warmth, water purification, signaling, cooking, psychological comfort, and protection from insects and predators. It is a fundamental survival tool.

### Fire Requirements: The Triangle

Every fire needs all three elements:
- **Fuel**: from finest (tinder) to medium (kindling) to largest (fuel wood). Collect all three before attempting to light a fire
- **Heat**: spark or flame to ignite the tinder
- **Oxygen**: fire must breathe — don't pack it too tightly, and don't smother it with large wood before it is established

### Collecting Fire Materials

**Tinder** (catches the first spark and burns instantly):
- Dry grass, dry leaves, dry pine needles
- Birch bark (peels into papery sheets, contains flammable oils, lights even slightly damp)
- Cedar bark rubbed into fine fibers
- Dry moss
- Dry fungus (amadou from horse hoof fungus is legendary tinder — shave the inner orange layer)
- Dry bird and mouse nests
- Fine dry wood shavings from the dry inner core of dead wood

**Kindling** (small dry sticks that establish the fire before fuel is added):
- Dry twigs pencil-width and smaller
- Split wood (inner surfaces are drier than outer)
- Dry pine cones

**Fuel wood** (sustains the fire):
- Dense hardwood burns slower and hotter: oak, hickory, maple, ash
- Softwood burns fast: pine, spruce, cedar — useful for getting fire going quickly or for signal fires
- Avoid green (living) wood — it smokes heavily and is hard to light
- Avoid wood with the bark fallen off lying on the ground — it has absorbed moisture

### Teepee Fire Lay

The most efficient fire lay for starting fires:
1. Build a small tinder nest on a dry base (bark, leaves)
2. Place kindling in a teepee (cone) shape around and above the tinder, with progressively larger pieces toward the outside
3. Leave a gap in the teepee facing the prevailing wind — this becomes your ignition opening
4. Light the tinder through the gap at the bottom, at the wind-facing side
5. Once the teepee is burning, add progressively larger fuel wood

### Starting Fire With Limited Tools

**Ferrocerium rod (ferro rod / firestarter):**
- Strike downward firmly and fast along the rod's length, angling sparks toward the tinder
- Hold the striker still and push the rod away from the tinder — this prevents scattering the tinder with the striker's movement
- Works wet, at altitude, and in cold — far more reliable than matches in the field

**Waterproof matches:**
- Strike on the box's striker surface (not the match head — this is common and wastes matches)
- Cup your hand behind the lit match against wind
- Store in a waterproof container (a lighter cap over the match head, a waterproof case, or a sealed plastic bag)

**Lighter:**
- The most reliable fire-starting tool. A single BIC lighter contains approximately 3,000 sparks
- Cold temperatures reduce the vapor pressure of butane — warm a cold lighter in your hands or armpit before use
- A lighter that runs out of fuel can still throw sparks and light fine tinder

**Friction fire (bow drill method) — no tools needed:**
This requires practice before a crisis. The principle: a spindle is rotated against a fireboard by a bow, generating a coal in a notch at the bottom.

1. **The bow**: a flexible branch about 60cm long with a bowstring (paracord, shoelace, vine, any cordage) tied loosely enough to wrap once around the spindle
2. **The spindle**: a straight, dry stick about 30cm long and 2cm diameter, with a blunt point at the bottom and a rounded top. Smooth but not polished
3. **The fireboard**: a flat dry piece of wood with a small depression for the spindle. Cut a V-shaped notch from the edge of the board to the center of the depression — the notch catches the coal
4. **The handhold**: a smooth depression in a piece of harder wood, green wood, stone, or bone — held in the non-dominant hand to keep pressure on the spindle top
5. **The coal catcher**: a piece of bark, leaf, or any flat material placed under the notch to catch the coal

**Method**: kneel with one knee on the fireboard to hold it still. Wrap the bowstring once around the spindle. Place the spindle in the depression, hold the handhold on top with your non-dominant hand, brace that arm against your shin. Saw the bow back and forth in long, smooth strokes while applying downward pressure. After 30–60 seconds of vigorous bowing, a coal will form in the notch. Tap it out onto your coal catcher, carry it to your tinder nest, fold the nest around it, and blow gently until it ignites.

**Wood selection for friction fire**: must be dry (critical), same hardness for spindle and board, and of medium density. Cedar, willow, basswood, cottonwood, mullein, and yucca work well. Avoid very hard wood (oak, maple) and very soft wood (pine resin clogs the hole).

### Fire Safety

- Build fire away from shelters, dry leaves, and overhanging branches
- Keep fire small when you only need heat — a small hot fire is more efficient than a large bonfire
- Never leave a fire unattended in dry conditions
- Extinguish fire completely: pour water, stir the ashes, add more water, feel the coals with your hand — they should not be warm
- In strong wind: build a stone windbreak or trench fire (fire in a narrow trench limits spark spread)

## Part 4: Water Procurement and Purification

You can survive approximately 3 days without water — less in heat, exertion, or illness. Dehydration causes progressive impairment of decision-making before you feel severely ill.

### Finding Water

**Terrain reading:**
- Water flows downhill and accumulates in valleys, low ground, and the outside bend of rivers and streams
- Green vegetation in otherwise dry terrain almost always indicates water within reach of plant roots — dig near tree bases
- Rock crevices and cliff bases accumulate seeping groundwater even in dry seasons
- Animal trails converge on water — follow them, especially at dawn and dusk when animals move toward water
- Insects swarm around water sources, particularly in large numbers at dusk

**Collection:**
- Rain is the safest source — collect from any clean surface using tarps, leaves, clothing, bark
- Morning dew: wipe absorbent cloth over vegetation before sunrise and wring into a container
- Transpiration bags: tie a clear plastic bag over leafy green branches, sealed at the branch — the plant exhales water vapor that condenses in the bag

**Sourcing from vegetation:**
- Many plants store water in their leaves, stems, and roots — see environment-specific guides for details on cacti, vines, bamboo, and tropical plants
- Tree hollows sometimes collect rainwater — inspect carefully and purify before drinking

### Water Purification

**Always purify water from natural sources unless it is clearly falling rain or collected directly from a sealed container. Clear water is not safe water.**

**Boiling** (most reliable):
- Rolling boil for 1 minute kills all biological pathogens
- At altitude above 2000m: boil 3 minutes (lower boiling point reduces killing efficiency)
- Does not remove chemical contamination

**Chemical tablets:**
- Iodine: 1 tablet per liter of clear water, 30 minutes contact time. Less effective in cold water — double time
- Chlorine dioxide: most effective chemical method, works in cold and turbid water, 4-hour wait for maximum effectiveness. Does not impart iodine taste
- Household bleach (sodium hypochlorite 5–6%): 2 drops per liter of clear water, 30 minutes

**Improvised filter** (reduces turbidity, does not kill pathogens — always follow with boiling):
- Layer sand, charcoal, and cloth in a container with a hole at the bottom
- Filter from top to bottom
- Charcoal must be from a fire — not ash, but the dark, brittle carbon pieces from the fire's edge

## Part 5: Signaling for Rescue

You are most likely to be rescued when you make yourself easy to find. Most people are recovered within 72 hours of a search beginning if they are in the search area and signaling effectively.

### The Rule of Three

The international distress signal is any signal repeated three times:
- 3 whistle blasts, pause, repeat
- 3 gunshots fired in sequence
- 3 fires in a triangle
- 3 flashes with a mirror or light
- 3 smoke columns visible from the air

### Signal Mirrors

A signal mirror is the single most effective long-range daytime signaling tool:
- Sunlight can be reflected 50+ kilometers in good conditions
- Use any reflective surface: a compact mirror, foil, polished metal, a CD
- Aim: hold the mirror near your face, extend your other hand toward the target (aircraft, search team), and tilt the mirror until the reflected sunlight falls on your extended hand. You are now signaling the target

### Ground-to-Air Signals

When aircraft are searching:
- Create large symbols in the ground that contrast strongly with their background — dark rocks on light sand, bright clothing on green grass, trampled patterns in snow
- Minimum size: 10 meters in each direction to be visible from aircraft altitude
- Standard symbols: **V** = need help; **X** = need medical assistance; **→** = traveling this direction; **LL** = all okay

### Smoke and Fire

- Daytime: smoke is visible for many kilometers. Generate contrasting smoke: dark smoke (rubber, oil, plastic, green vegetation) against light sky; white smoke (green leaves on fire, adding water to coals) against dark forest background
- Nighttime: fire is visible many kilometers. Three fires in a triangle is unmistakable
- Build signal fires in open areas: hilltops, clearings, beaches, roadsides — not under dense tree canopy

### Whistle

A whistle carries 2–3× farther than a human voice, requires almost no energy, and can be heard over background noise. Three blasts, pause, three blasts.

## Part 6: Navigation Without Technology

### Compass Use

If you have a compass, orient the red needle to North, then rotate the housing dial until the North marker aligns with the red needle. The direction you need to travel is then readable from the housing. Navigate by picking a distant landmark in your intended direction and walking to it, then picking the next landmark.

### Natural Navigation

**Sun:**
- The sun rises roughly in the East and sets roughly in the West everywhere on Earth (not exactly, but close enough for coarse navigation)
- At solar noon (when shadows are shortest), the sun is due South in the Northern Hemisphere and due North in the Southern Hemisphere
- Shadow stick: push a stick in level ground, mark the shadow tip, wait 15 minutes, mark again. The line from first to second mark points East-West (first mark is West, Northern Hemisphere)

**Stars:**
- North Star (Polaris): find the Big Dipper, the two outer "pointer" stars of the cup extend directly to Polaris. Polaris is always within 1 degree of true North
- Southern Cross: for Southern Hemisphere. Extend the long axis of the cross 4.5 times its length to find approximate South

**Moon:**
- A crescent moon's two horns point toward the sun — draw a line through them to the horizon for an approximate South point (Northern Hemisphere)

### Maintaining Direction

- Pick a distant landmark in your intended direction and walk toward it. When you reach it, pick the next landmark in the same direction
- In low visibility: walk with the sun or wind consistently on one side of your body
- Leave trail markers if there is any chance of doubling back (search and rescue teams will also use your trail)

## Part 7: Emergency Food Procurement

Food is the last survival priority — you have weeks before starvation becomes critical. However, maintaining caloric intake preserves physical and mental capacity.

### Universal Edibility Test

Use this to test any unfamiliar plant. Takes 8 hours but prevents poisoning:
1. Test one plant part at a time (leaf, stem, root, fruit — separately)
2. Crush and hold against inner wrist/elbow for 15 minutes — observe for reaction
3. Hold to lips 3 minutes, observe
4. On tongue 15 minutes without swallowing, observe
5. Chew without swallowing 15 minutes, then spit — observe
6. Wait 8 hours without eating. If no nausea or ill effects: it is likely safe

### Reliable Food Sources

**Insects**: nearly universal, high in protein and fat, almost always safe to eat cooked. Avoid brightly colored insects, hairy caterpillars, and stinging insects.

**Small animals**: squirrels, rabbits, birds, frogs, and fish are calorie-dense and far safer than most wild plants. Cook everything thoroughly — raw meat and fish carry parasites.

**Eggs**: bird eggs found in nests. All bird eggs are edible at any stage of development.

**Inner bark**: the cambium layer (soft inner bark) of pine, birch, willow, and many deciduous trees is edible raw or cooked. Scrape off the outer bark, then strip the soft layer underneath. Pine cambium is mildly sweet, high in vitamin C.

**Roots and tubers**: many plants store calories underground in starchy roots and tubers. Apply the edibility test before consuming.

### Fishing and Trapping

**Fishing without gear:**
- Improvise hook from wire, safety pin, thorn, or carved bone
- Line from paracord, vines, shoelace, or any thin cordage
- Bait with worms, insects, torn fabric, anything shiny
- Position in calm water near structure (submerged logs, undercut banks) at dawn or dusk

**Simple snare for small animals:**
- Find an active trail (look for tracks, droppings, hair on brush)
- Tie a loop of cordage (paracord, wire) about 10cm diameter, suspended 7–10cm above the ground on the animal's trail
- Attach the loop to a heavy branch or stake in the ground — the loop must be fixed so the animal cannot escape
- Check every 4–6 hours — a trapped animal will alert predators if left too long

**Always cook everything thoroughly** — raw wild meat and fish carry bacteria and parasites that can incapacitate you quickly.

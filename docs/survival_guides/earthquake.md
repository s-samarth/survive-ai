# Earthquake and Building Collapse (India)

Around 59% of India's land area is vulnerable to moderate or severe earthquakes. Zone V — the highest risk — covers Kashmir, Himachal, Uttarakhand's higher reaches, the whole North East, north Bihar, and the Kutch region of Gujarat. Zone IV covers Delhi NCR, much of the Indo-Gangetic plain, and parts of Maharashtra. Delhi sits on Zone IV with dense unreinforced construction — it is the highest-consequence urban risk in the country.

Indian earthquake deaths are almost entirely building deaths. The ground rarely kills you; the building does.

---

## Part 1: During the Shaking

Shaking usually lasts 10-60 seconds. You do not have time to plan. Act on reflex.

**Indoors — Drop, Cover, Hold On:**
1. **Drop** to hands and knees immediately. This stops you being thrown down.
2. **Cover** your head and neck. Get under a sturdy table or desk. If there is none, get against an **interior** wall away from windows, and cover your head with your arms, a pillow, a bag, or a helmet.
3. **Hold on** to the table leg and move with it if it shifts.

**Stay inside during the shaking.** In Indian cities, the space immediately outside a building is the most dangerous place on earth during a quake — falling parapets, balcony slabs, water tanks, AC units, hoardings, glass, and electric poles all land there. Most Indian earthquake injuries happen to people who ran out.

**The "triangle of life" advice is wrong.** Do not lie down beside furniture instead of under it. Every engineering body that has studied this rejects it. Get under something.

**Exception — single-storey, mud, or unreinforced masonry (kutcha) houses:** these collapse completely rather than pancaking, and there is nothing inside to shelter under. If you are within two or three steps of the door and there is open ground outside, get out. Otherwise get under the strongest table you have and protect your head.

**In bed:** stay there. Turn face down and cover your head and neck with a pillow. More people are injured crossing a dark room full of broken glass than are hurt by the ceiling.

**In a high-rise:** drop, cover, hold on. **Do not run for the stairs during shaking** — you will be thrown down them. Do not use the lift. Modern Indian high-rises built to IS 1893 are designed to sway and survive; they will feel terrifying and be structurally fine.

**Outdoors:** move to open ground away from buildings, walls, hoardings, trees, and power lines. Drop and cover your head. In dense Indian streets there may be no open ground — in that case, stand in a doorway of the strongest-looking building or press against an interior wall away from windows and overhead projections.

**In a vehicle:** pull over away from flyovers, bridges, underpasses, hoardings, and power lines. Stay inside with the seatbelt on. The car's structure protects you from falling debris.

**In a crowd or market:** the crush is a bigger threat than the quake. Get to the edge, protect your head, and stay off the ground. See the **Crowd and Stampede** guide.

## Part 2: The First Ten Minutes After

1. **Expect aftershocks.** They begin within seconds and can be nearly as strong as the main shock. Buildings weakened by the main shock fall in the aftershocks — this is when most rescuers die.
2. **Check yourself, then others.** Adrenaline hides injuries.
3. **Put on shoes before you take a step.** Broken glass is everywhere. Foot lacerations are the most common post-quake injury and they cripple you exactly when you need to walk.
4. **Smell for gas.** If you smell LPG or PNG: do not switch anything on or off, do not use a lighter or a matchstick, do not use your phone in the room. Open windows, close the cylinder regulator or the main gas valve, and leave.
5. **Cut the electricity at the main switch** if you can reach it safely and dry.
6. **Check the doorframe before you open the door.** If the frame is visibly bowed or racked, the wall is carrying a load it was not designed for. Open carefully. Be ready for the door to be jammed — that means the frame has deformed and the structure is compromised.
7. **Do not use the lift.** Ever, after a quake.
8. **Get out and get to open ground** if the structure shows any of the danger signs below.

## Part 3: Reading a Damaged Building

Learn these signs. They decide whether you go back in.

**Get out and stay out if you see:**
- **X-shaped diagonal cracks in walls or columns** — this is shear failure. The building has lost lateral strength.
- **Cracks in columns, especially near the top or bottom where they meet the beam.** Column damage is far more serious than wall damage.
- **Exposed or buckled reinforcement bars** in a column
- **Concrete crushed or spalled off** a column, exposing the steel
- **The building visibly leaning**, or a floor that is no longer level
- **A soft storey** that has partly collapsed — extremely common in Indian buildings with open parking or shopfronts on the ground floor and heavy floors above
- **Doors and windows that will not close** because the frames have deformed
- **New gaps between the building and adjacent structures**, or between the staircase and the walls
- **Continuous creaking, ticking, or dust falling** from the ceiling

**Probably survivable to re-enter briefly:**
- Fine hairline cracks in plaster only
- Cracks in non-load-bearing partition walls that do not extend into the structure
- Fallen false ceiling

**When in doubt, stay out.** Sleep in the open, in a vehicle, or in a tent. Every Indian earthquake produces deaths of people who went back into a cracked house for belongings and were killed by an aftershock.

## Part 4: If You Are Trapped Under Rubble

You can survive for days. People have been pulled alive from Indian collapses after 3-5 days. Your job is to stay alive and be findable.

**Immediately:**
1. **Do not light anything.** Gas leaks are almost universal in collapse voids.
2. **Cover your mouth and nose** with cloth. Pulverised concrete dust is the biggest immediate threat — it causes silicosis-like lung injury within hours and it is what kills many people who survived the collapse itself.
3. **Do not move suddenly or thrash.** You may bring more material down or shift the void you are in. Move one limb at a time, slowly, and feel what is above you.
4. **Do not shout continuously.** Shouting fills your lungs with dust, dehydrates you, and exhausts you within an hour.

**Signalling — this is your main job:**
- **Tap on concrete, rebar, or pipe in groups of three.** Metal on metal carries furthest. Use a stone, a shoe heel, a piece of rebar.
- **Tap for 2 minutes, then listen in silence for 2 minutes.** Repeat. Rescue teams work in listening cycles and call for total silence on the site — they are listening for exactly this.
- **Tap most at dawn and after dark**, when ambient noise is lowest and listening operations are most likely.
- **Use a whistle if you have one.** Almost no energy cost.
- **Save your phone battery.** Airplane mode. Try one SMS every hour or two — SMS gets through concrete better than a voice call.

**Staying alive:**
- **Water first.** Look for a burst pipe, a geyser tank, a cistern, a water bottle, condensation on a metal or plastic surface. Ration nothing until you have found a source — the myth that you should sip slowly costs lives; drink when thirsty, but do not gulp a whole supply at once.
- **Do not drink urine, sewage, or standing water in a collapse void.**
- **Stay still and stay covered.** Movement burns water. Cover yourself with cloth or debris for insulation — collapse voids get cold at night even in Indian summer.
- **Urinate away from where you are lying.** Sanitation matters over days.
- **Do not sleep in a position where a limb is crushed under weight.**

**Crush syndrome — critically important:** if a heavy weight has been on a limb for over 1-2 hours, do not let anyone lift it off you until medical help with an IV drip is present. When the weight comes off, potassium and toxins from the crushed muscle flood into the circulation and can stop the heart within minutes. Tell rescuers clearly: **"my leg has been crushed for X hours."** If lifting is unavoidable, a tourniquet applied above the crush before the weight is lifted can buy time.

## Part 5: If You Are Rescuing Others

Do not become a casualty. Untrained rescuers are a large share of secondary deaths.

- **Call for help and mark the location first.** Note the exact building, floor, and where the voice came from. Write it on the wall outside.
- **Do not enter a partially collapsed structure.** Work from the outside and from the top of the debris pile.
- **Listen before you dig.** Get everyone silent. Shout "if you can hear me, tap three times." Then listen for a full minute.
- **Remove debris from the top down, one piece at a time.** Never pull a piece out from the middle or the bottom of a pile — the pile is an arch and you will collapse it onto the person.
- **Never lift a slab off someone without a plan for what holds it.** Crib it — build a stack of bricks or wood on both sides as you lift, so it cannot come back down.
- **Get water and air to them before you get them out.** A tube, a pipe, a bottle passed through a gap keeps them alive for the hours a proper extraction takes.
- **Concrete dust:** wear an N95 or a wet cloth. Wet the debris with water to suppress dust if you have any to spare.
- **Do not use heavy machinery (JCB) on a pile with live victims.** This has killed survivors in multiple Indian collapses.

**When NDRF or SDRF arrive, hand over.** Tell them: how many people, where exactly, when you last heard them, what condition they were in, and where the gas and electrical mains are. Then get out of their way.

## Part 6: Tsunami After an Earthquake

If you are on the coast — Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Odisha, Kerala, West Bengal, Andaman and Nicobar, Lakshadweep — and you feel an earthquake strong enough that you cannot stand comfortably, or you see the sea suddenly withdraw exposing the seabed:

**Do not wait for a warning. Run inland and uphill immediately.** The 2004 tsunami reached Tamil Nadu about 2 hours after the Sumatra quake, but a local quake in the Andaman-Nicobar arc gives only minutes.

## Part 7: The Days After

- **Sleep outside or in a verified-safe structure** for at least 72 hours, longer if aftershocks continue.
- **Boil or chlorinate all water.** Pipes crack in a quake and sewage mixes into the supply. Waterborne outbreaks follow every Indian earthquake.
- **Register yourself** at the relief camp, hospital, or police station so you come off missing lists and so your family can find you.
- **Do not go sightseeing at collapse sites.** You obstruct rescue and you are at risk from aftershock collapse.
- **Watch for gas and electrical hazards** for weeks — damaged wiring and gas lines cause a second wave of fires.
- **Expect psychological effects.** Sleeplessness, hypervigilance, feeling phantom shaking, and children regressing are normal for weeks. See the **Vulnerable People** guide.

## Part 8: Preparing Before

- **Identify your safe spots** in every room now: under which table, against which interior wall.
- **Anchor tall furniture** — almirahs, bookshelves, and TVs — to the wall. In Indian homes the steel almirah is a common crush killer.
- **Do not sleep with heavy objects, glass, or a mirror above the bed.**
- **Keep footwear and a torch under the bed** in a bag tied to the bed leg so it does not slide away.
- **Know where your gas regulator and electrical main switch are**, and be able to reach them in the dark.
- **Know your building's construction.** If you live in a soft-storey building (open parking below, flats above) in Zone IV or V, get it retrofitted — this single feature accounts for a large share of Indian collapses.
- **Agree a family rally point** outside and away from the building.

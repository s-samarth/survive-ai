# Children, Pregnancy, Elderly and Disability in an Emergency (India)

In Indian disasters, the people who die disproportionately are not the strongest-hit — they are the ones who could not run, could not be carried, could not access a shelter, or had no one to notice they were missing. This guide is about them.

---

## Part 1: Infants and Small Children

**Physiology to understand:**
- Children dehydrate far faster than adults. A child can go from playing to critically dehydrated in a few hours in Indian heat.
- Children lose body heat much faster — a large head relative to the body and less fat. A child gets hypothermic at temperatures an adult finds mild.
- Children breathe faster, so they inhale proportionally more smoke, gas, and dust.
- Children are shorter, so in a gas release they are deeper in the heavy vapour layer. **Pick them up.**

**Dehydration in a child — danger signs:**
- Fewer than 6 wet nappies a day, or no urine for 6 hours
- No tears when crying
- Dry mouth and tongue
- Sunken eyes, or a sunken soft spot (fontanelle) on an infant's head
- Skin that stays tented when pinched
- **Lethargy, floppiness, or difficulty waking — this is an emergency**

**Feeding:**
- **Keep breastfeeding. Do not stop, ever, in an emergency.** Breast milk is clean, warm, free, immunologically protective, and requires no water. Stress does not stop milk production — dehydration and not feeding do. Feed more often, not less. A mother who thinks her milk has "dried up" from shock almost always has milk; frequent suckling restores flow.
- **Do not accept donated infant formula in relief settings unless there is no alternative and clean water is guaranteed.** Formula made with contaminated water kills infants. This is a well-documented harm in Indian and international relief operations.
- If formula is unavoidable, use boiled water, sterilise bottles by boiling, and make each feed fresh. **Cup feeding is safer than bottle feeding** when sterilisation is uncertain — a cup can be cleaned properly, a teat cannot.
- For a child over 6 months: continue normal food. **Do not withhold food during diarrhoea** — this prolongs it. Khichdi, curd, banana, rice water, mashed dal.
- **ORS and zinc for diarrhoea.** See the **Water, Food and Sanitation** guide.

**Protection:**
- **Write your phone number and the child's name on their forearm in permanent marker** before entering any crowd, camp, or evacuation. This is standard practice at Kumbh and it works.
- **Attach a card to every child's bag** with name, parents' names, address, phone numbers, blood group, and allergies.
- **Never carry a child on your shoulders in a dense crowd.** Carry them on your chest, held high, facing you. See the **Crowd and Stampede** guide.
- **Hold a child's wrist, not their hand.** Hands slip apart.
- **Brief them:** "If we get separated, stay exactly where you are and shout my name. Do not go looking for me. If you need help, go to a woman with children, a policeman, or a shopkeeper."
- **Child trafficking spikes after Indian disasters.** Never hand a child to anyone without official identification and a written record. Register every unaccompanied child with the police, the **Childline helpline 1098**, or the district Child Welfare Committee. Do not let an unaccompanied child be taken away by a stranger, however helpful they seem.

**Psychological:**
- Expect **regression** — bedwetting, thumb sucking, clinginess, baby talk — after a disaster. This is normal and temporary.
- Expect nightmares, fear of being alone, fear of the dark, silence, and repetitive play about the event.
- **What helps:** routine, physical closeness, honest and simple answers, letting them talk or draw about it, limiting their exposure to repeated news footage, and keeping them with familiar adults.
- **What harms:** telling them nothing, telling them not to cry, separating them from caregivers, and forcing them to talk before they are ready.
- Persistent symptoms beyond a month need help. **Tele-MANAS: 14416** and **Childline: 1098**.

## Part 2: Pregnancy and Childbirth

**Pregnant women in an Indian emergency face three risks: obstructed labour with no facility, haemorrhage, and infection.**

**Priorities:**
- **Identify every pregnant woman in a shelter or a group immediately** and note her due date. Prioritise her for evacuation, shade, water, and food.
- **Hepatitis E, which spreads through contaminated water, has very high mortality in pregnancy.** Pregnant women must get the cleanest water available, boiled.
- **She needs more water, more food, and more rest than anyone else in the group.**
- **Iron and folic acid** should continue if available.

**Danger signs — get to a facility urgently:**
- Bleeding from the vagina at any stage
- Severe headache, blurred vision, swelling of the face and hands, or fits — **this is pre-eclampsia/eclampsia and it kills quickly**
- Severe abdominal pain
- Fever
- Waters breaking before 37 weeks, or waters that are green or foul-smelling
- Reduced or absent fetal movement
- Labour lasting more than 12 hours without progress

**If you must assist a delivery with no medical help:**
1. **Wash your hands thoroughly with soap.** Use gloves if you have them, or clean plastic bags over your hands.
2. **Clean space, clean cloth, clean blade.** A new razor blade or scissors boiled for 20 minutes.
3. **Let her adopt whatever position she wants.** Squatting and side-lying are often easier than lying flat.
4. **Do not pull the baby. Do not push on the abdomen.** Support the head as it emerges and let the body follow with the contractions.
5. **If the cord is around the neck**, gently slip it over the head.
6. **Dry the baby immediately and vigorously with a clean cloth** — this stimulates breathing. **Then put the baby skin-to-skin on the mother's chest and cover both.** Drying, warming, and skin-to-skin are the three actions that save newborn lives.
7. **If the baby does not breathe:** dry and stimulate by rubbing the back. If still not breathing after 30 seconds, give gentle rescue breaths — small puffs from your cheeks, 30-40 per minute, just enough to see the chest rise.
8. **Delay cutting the cord for 1-3 minutes** — this transfers significant blood to the baby. Then tie firmly in two places with clean string, and cut between the ties.
9. **The placenta should deliver within 30 minutes.** **Do not pull the cord.** Let it come. Keep it for the health worker to check that it is complete.
10. **Massage the uterus firmly through the abdomen after delivery** — a hard, contracted uterus stops bleeding. A soft, boggy one is bleeding. **Put the baby to the breast immediately** — suckling releases oxytocin and contracts the uterus. These two actions are the main defence against post-partum haemorrhage.
11. **Heavy bleeding after delivery is the leading cause of maternal death in India.** Massage the uterus continuously, keep the baby feeding, lie her flat with legs raised, and get to a facility.
12. **Keep both mother and baby warm.** Newborns get hypothermic in minutes, even in Indian weather.
13. **Nothing is to be inserted into the vagina.** No traditional preparations on the cord stump — this is how neonatal tetanus happens.

**Helpline: 102** in most states is the free ambulance service specifically for pregnant women and infants (Janani Express / JSSK).

## Part 3: Elderly People

**Elderly people are the largest death group in Indian heatwaves and cold waves, and among the highest in floods and fires.** The reasons are consistent: they do not feel thirst or temperature change as strongly, they take medications that impair heat regulation, they cannot move quickly, and they live alone.

**Practical actions:**
- **Check on elderly neighbours at least twice a day during a heatwave, a cold wave, or any emergency.** They will not ask. Isolation is the main risk factor.
- **Do not wait for them to say they are thirsty.** Offer water on a schedule.
- **Their medications matter more than yours.** Diuretics, beta-blockers, antihypertensives, insulin, thyroid medication, anticoagulants, and psychiatric drugs cannot be stopped abruptly. **Keep 30 days in stock and a written prescription.**
- **Diabetics** need food on schedule. A missed meal with insulin taken causes hypoglycaemia — confusion, sweating, aggression, collapse. Give sugar immediately.
- **Dialysis patients** are the most time-critical group in any Indian disaster. Identify them early, and prioritise their evacuation. Missing sessions is fatal within days.
- **They need help to evacuate, and they need it early.** Assign a specific named person to each elderly resident in your building or lane. "Someone will help" means nobody does.
- **Confusion in an elderly person during a crisis is often dehydration, infection, low sugar, or a missed medication — not dementia.** Check for those first.
- **Falls** are the most common injury. Clear the route, provide light, and provide an arm.
- **Do not separate an elderly person with dementia from a familiar carer.** Disorientation in an unfamiliar shelter causes wandering and death.

## Part 4: People with Disabilities

- **Ask before you help. Ask how to help.** The person knows their own body and equipment better than you do.
- **Wheelchair users:** never lift a wheelchair by its removable parts (armrests, footplates). Ask about transfer technique. In an evacuation, the wheelchair usually cannot go down stairs — plan for a carry and for the chair to be brought separately or replaced.
- **Blind or low-vision:** offer your arm and let them take it, walk half a step ahead, describe the route and hazards out loud ("three steps down, then a turn right, wet floor"). **Do not grab or push them.** Do not move their belongings or their cane without telling them.
- **Deaf or hard of hearing:** get their attention visually, face them in good light, speak clearly without exaggerating, write things down. Sirens and public announcements do not reach them — **this is the single biggest gap in Indian disaster warning and it is why deaf people die in floods and fires.** Assign someone to inform them directly.
- **Intellectual and psychosocial disability:** keep the familiar carer with them, use simple concrete language, one instruction at a time, maintain routine, and give advance warning of changes.
- **Assistive devices are not optional.** Hearing aids, spectacles, crutches, prosthetics, catheters, and a wheelchair are as essential as medicine. Get them out with the person, and keep spare batteries.
- **Guide dogs and service animals** go with the person. Do not separate them.
- **Camps and shelters in India are usually not accessible.** Advocate specifically: an accessible toilet, a ground-floor space, a ramp, and a lit path. Say it to the camp in-charge explicitly, because nobody else will.

## Part 5: Women's Safety

**Violence against women rises measurably after Indian disasters** — in camps, in displaced settlements, and during blackouts.

- **Do not sleep alone in an open shelter.** Group with other women and families.
- **Toilets and bathing areas in camps must be separate, lockable, and lit, and women should go in pairs, especially at night.** If they are not, raise it with the camp in-charge and the police immediately.
- **Keep children, especially girls, physically with a trusted adult at all times.**
- **Menstrual supplies, a private space, water and soap** must be requested explicitly. They are routinely omitted from Indian relief distributions.
- **Report harassment to the police desk at the camp, or 112, or the women's helpline 181 or 1091.** Note names and times.
- **A woman's ID and documents are her access to relief, compensation, and her own bank account.** Keep them with her, not with a male relative.
- **Trafficking recruiters operate at Indian disaster sites**, offering jobs, marriage, or transport to women and girls. **Never let anyone take a woman or a girl away without official documentation and a record of who took them where.**

## Part 6: Mental Health

Everything below is a **normal reaction to an abnormal event**, not a weakness or an illness.

**Expect, in the first weeks:** difficulty sleeping, nightmares, replaying the event, hypervigilance and jumpiness, irritability and anger, numbness, guilt at having survived, difficulty concentrating, appetite changes, unexplained aches, and avoidance of reminders.

**What helps:**
- **Safety, food, water, sleep, and information first.** Psychological support without these is useless.
- **Reconnect people with their families.** Separation is the strongest predictor of long-term harm.
- **Restore routine** — fixed mealtimes, fixed sleep times, work, and school. Getting children back to schooling, even informally in a camp, has a large measurable effect.
- **Let people talk if they want to, and do not force them if they do not.** "Debriefing" people who are not ready causes harm.
- **Practical help is psychological help.** Helping someone find a document, get water, or trace a relative does more than any conversation.
- **Give people something useful to do.**
- **Limit repeated exposure to disaster footage**, especially for children.

**Seek help when:** symptoms are severe or persist beyond a month, someone cannot function, someone is drinking heavily, or **anyone talks about wanting to die or shows no interest in surviving.**

**Helplines in India:**
- **Tele-MANAS: 14416** or 1800-891-4416 — the free national 24x7 mental health service, available in multiple Indian languages
- **KIRAN: 1800-599-0019** — national mental health rehabilitation helpline
- **Childline: 1098** — for children
- **Women's helpline: 181** and **1091**
- **iCall: 9152987821** and **AASRA: 9820466726** — counselling and suicide prevention

**If someone talks about suicide:** take it seriously, stay with them, listen without judgement, remove obvious means (pesticides, ropes, medicines — **pesticide ingestion is the leading suicide method in rural India, so secure it**), and get them to a doctor or a helpline. Do not leave them alone. Do not promise to keep it secret.

# Riots, Curfew and Civil Unrest (India)

Communal violence, caste conflict, protest crackdowns, mob violence, and lathi charges are recurring realities across India. Internet shutdowns and Section 144 / BNSS prohibitory orders almost always accompany them, so this guide should be read together with the **Communication Blackout** guide.

The overriding principle: **your goal is to be absent, not to be brave.** Nothing you own is worth being in a crowd during communal violence.

---

## Part 1: Early Warning Signs

Violence rarely starts without hours or days of signals. Learn to read them.

- **A sudden internet or mobile-data shutdown** with no storm and no power cut. This is the state anticipating or responding to violence. Go home.
- **Section 144 CrPC / BNSS 163 prohibitory orders** announced for your area
- Shops closing early, en masse, without explanation. Shopkeepers know first.
- **Unusual gatherings of young men**, especially carrying sticks, flags, or stones
- Provocative processions, loud sloganeering, or a religious procession routed through a mixed neighbourhood
- A rumour spreading fast on WhatsApp about a killing, a desecration, a kidnapping, or a "love jihad" allegation. **Rumour is the fuel of Indian communal violence.** Do not forward it. Do not act on it.
- Police deployment increasing, or conversely, police visibly withdrawing
- Schools announcing sudden closure
- Neighbours from one community leaving the area

**When you see two or more of these, get home, get everyone inside, and stay there.**

## Part 2: If You Are Caught Outside

**Get off the street. Immediately.**

1. **Do not run through the crowd.** Running attracts attention and marks you as a target or as fleeing prey. Walk purposefully, at a normal pace, at a right angle to the crowd's direction.
2. **Move to a side lane, then away.** Main roads are where mobs travel and where police disperse them.
3. **Get inside anything with a shutter or a door.** A shop, a house, a hotel, a hospital, a religious building, a bank, a government office. In India, asking politely at a door is usually enough — most people will let a family in. **Hospitals and police stations are the two places least likely to be attacked.**
4. **Get off the ground floor.** Upper floors are safer from both stone-throwing and fire.
5. **Do not be identifiable as belonging to any side.** Remove or cover caps, tilaks, skull caps, party scarves, flags, badges, and religious markers if there is any risk. This is not cowardice; it is survival. Say nothing about your religion, caste, or politics.
6. **Do not film openly.** Being seen filming a mob makes you a target for that mob, and being seen filming police makes you a target for police. If you must document, do it from behind glass, from height, or discreetly, and back it up off the device.
7. **Do not stop to help a stranger in the middle of a mob** unless you can do so without entering the mob. This is a terrible thing to write. It is also true.

## Part 3: In a Vehicle

- **Do not drive into or through a crowd.** Reverse out or take any side road.
- **Lock the doors, close the windows, keep moving slowly and steadily.** Do not stop. Do not accelerate into people.
- **If the vehicle is surrounded and stopped:** do not get out while you can move. Keep moving at walking pace with continuous horn. If you are trapped and being attacked, **get out and run** — a vehicle set on fire is a death trap and cars are common targets in Indian riots.
- **Remove identifying stickers** — party symbols, religious symbols, state registration plates that mark you as an outsider — if you routinely travel through sensitive areas.
- **Two-wheelers are more vulnerable than cars** but can escape through gaps. Keep the helmet on; it is armour against stones.

## Part 4: Tear Gas, Lathi Charge and Water Cannon

**Lathi charge:**
- **Move away at a right angle, do not run directly away.** Police lines advance in a direction; you leave sideways.
- **Do not fall.** Being on the ground during a lathi charge causes the most serious injuries.
- **Protect your head.** A helmet, a bag held over your head, or your forearms.
- **Do not push back or grab a lathi.** This escalates instantly and is a criminal offence.
- **Do not be at the front of a crowd facing police.** Ever.

**Tear gas (CS gas):**
- **Do not rub your eyes.** Rubbing grinds the crystals into the skin and eyes and makes it far worse.
- **Move upwind and uphill.** The gas is heavier than air and settles low. Get to height and to moving air.
- **Blink rapidly and let your eyes stream.** Tears flush it out.
- **Flush with lots of plain water or saline.** Water works. The "milk" and "Coca-Cola" remedies commonly used at Indian protests are not better than plenty of clean water and carry infection risk.
- **Face into the wind with your eyes open and arms out** for a minute — this is uncomfortable but clears the fastest.
- **Do not use oil-based creams or moisturiser on your skin** beforehand; the chemical dissolves into oil and stays on the skin.
- **A wet cloth over the nose and mouth helps a little.** A properly fitted N95 helps more. Swimming goggles or sealed spectacles protect the eyes.
- **Remove contact lenses immediately** and do not put them back.
- **Once out, remove and bag your outer clothing**, shower in cool water (hot water opens pores and reintroduces the chemical), and wash hair last.
- **A canister is hot and can maim.** Do not pick one up with bare hands. Do not throw it back — that is an offence and it may explode.

**Water cannon:** the jet can break bones and detach a retina. Turn your back, protect your head, do not face it, and move out of the line. Get out of wet clothing afterwards — hypothermia is a real risk in a north Indian winter.

**Rubber bullets and pellets:** not non-lethal. Aim for cover, keep low, protect your eyes and head. Pellet injuries to the eye are permanently blinding and have been widespread in Kashmir. If you are hit in the eye, cover **both** eyes with a loose shield (do not press), and get to an eye hospital urgently.

## Part 5: Sheltering at Home During Violence

1. **Lock and reinforce.** Bolt the main door, drop the shutter, and add anything heavy behind it. Lock the terrace door.
2. **Move everyone to an interior room** away from windows facing the street. Windows are where stones and petrol bombs come through.
3. **Switch off the outside lights.** Do not stand at a lit window at night — you are a silhouette.
4. **Keep the gas cylinder regulator off** and move it away from the door. Fire is the main mob weapon in Indian riots.
5. **Fill water containers now.** Supply is often cut, and you may need it to fight fire.
6. **Keep buckets of water and sand near the entrance** for a petrol bomb.
7. **Keep a wet cloth per person** for smoke.
8. **Have an escape route to the terrace and to a neighbour's terrace.** In dense Indian neighbourhoods, adjacent rooftops are the standard escape and have saved many families. Check the route in daylight.
9. **Do not confront a mob from a window or a balcony.** Do not shout back. Do not throw anything. Do not show a weapon.
10. **Coordinate with neighbours of all communities.** Mixed-community neighbourhood groups protecting each other's homes is the single most effective protection observed in Indian riots. Set up a WhatsApp group and a physical warning system (a whistle, banging a thali) now, before you need it.

## Part 6: Curfew

- **A curfew is a legal order. Violating it is an offence and it is dangerous.** Police have shoot-on-sight authority in extreme cases.
- **Curfew passes** are issued by the district magistrate or police commissioner for medical emergencies, essential services, and press. If someone in your household needs dialysis, chemotherapy, insulin, or is pregnant near term, **apply immediately** or move them to hospital before the curfew tightens.
- **Relaxation windows** are announced for buying essentials, usually a few hours. Go early, go with one person only, take cash, buy for a week, come straight back.
- **Carry ID always.** Aadhaar, and a hospital paper if you have a medical reason to be out.
- **If stopped:** be polite, keep your hands visible, do not argue, do not record openly, state your reason briefly, and comply. Argue later, through a lawyer.
- **Do not gather on the street even in your own lane.** Groups of four or more are what prohibitory orders prohibit.

## Part 7: If You Have to Leave

Evacuating during communal violence is a real and sometimes correct decision.

- **Go early, in daylight, before the roads close.** Waiting for it to get worse is how families get trapped.
- **Go to a relief camp, a police station, a hospital, or a large religious institution of your own community** — these are where organised protection concentrates.
- **Travel light and travel plain.** No visible valuables, no religious markers.
- **Take:** ID for everyone, cash, medicines, phone, charger, water, ORS, a change of clothes, and children's documents.
- **Tell an out-of-state relative where you are going** by SMS before you move.
- **Do not travel alone at night.**
- **Register at the camp** so you appear on official lists — this matters for both safety and later compensation.

---

## Part 8: Afterwards

- **Document everything** — photographs of damage, injuries, medical records, police complaint (FIR) copy, witness names. Compensation and prosecution both depend on this.
- **File an FIR** even if the police are reluctant. If a police station refuses, you can send a written complaint by registered post to the Superintendent of Police, and approach the magistrate under Section 156(3) CrPC / the corresponding BNSS provision.
- **Get medical documentation of every injury** from a government hospital. This is the evidentiary record that matters.
- **Contact the State Human Rights Commission or the NHRC**, and legal aid organisations, if the police are complicit or unresponsive. **NALSA legal aid is free** — the district legal services authority sits in every district court.
- **Do not post inflammatory content** afterwards. It prolongs violence and it is prosecutable.
- **Expect psychological effects in children** — regression, nightmares, clinginess, silence. See the **Vulnerable People** guide.

# Water, Food and Sanitation in a Cutoff (India)

After the first 24 hours of any Indian disaster, the threat shifts from the event itself to what follows: contaminated water, spoiled food, and failed sanitation. **Diarrhoeal disease has killed more people in Indian disasters than the disasters themselves.**

---

## Part 1: How Much Water You Actually Need

- **3 litres per person per day is the survival minimum** for drinking alone, in normal Indian conditions.
- **In summer heat (above 40 C), or with physical work, this doubles to 6 litres or more.**
- Add roughly 2-3 litres per person per day for cooking and basic hygiene.
- **Children, pregnant women, breastfeeding mothers, and the elderly need proportionally more and dehydrate faster.**
- **Do not ration drinking water below your need in order to make it last.** Dehydration impairs judgement within hours and that kills you faster than running out. Ration your activity and your exposure to heat instead — find shade, stay still, work at night.

**Signs you are dehydrated:** dark yellow urine; passing urine less than 3-4 times a day; dry mouth; headache; dizziness on standing; tiredness; irritability. In infants: fewer than 6 wet nappies a day, no tears when crying, sunken eyes, a sunken soft spot on the head, and lethargy.

## Part 2: Where to Find Water in an Indian Emergency

**Inside a building, in order of safety:**
1. **The overhead tank (Sintex/RCC tank on the roof)** — most Indian buildings hold 500-1000 litres up there. Usually still clean. This is your primary emergency reserve.
2. **The underground sump** — larger but more likely to be contaminated. Treat it.
3. **The geyser/water heater tank** — 10-25 litres of clean water. Switch off the power, let it cool, and drain from the bottom outlet.
4. **The toilet cistern** (the tank above, not the bowl) — clean if no chemical block is used.
5. **Ice in the freezer**, melted
6. **The water in pipes** — open the highest tap in the building to let air in, then drain from the lowest tap
7. **Water in a cooler, RO reject water (treat it), canned vegetables and dal liquid, coconut water**

**Outside:**
- **Rainwater** — the cleanest natural source in India. Catch it in the open with a clean sheet, tarpaulin, or vessel. **Discard the first few minutes of runoff from a roof** (it carries dust, bird droppings, and roof debris), then collect. Still worth treating.
- **Handpumps and borewells** — generally safer than surface water because the source is deep, but they get contaminated in floods when the water table rises over the wellhead. Treat anyway.
- **Rivers, tanks, ponds, and canals** — always treat. Take from a flowing part, upstream of habitation, away from banks.
- **Dew** — wipe grass or leaves with a cloth at dawn and wring it out. Surprisingly productive.
- **Transpiration bag** — tie a clear plastic bag over a leafy branch of a non-poisonous tree in the sun. Water condenses over several hours.
- **Do not drink:** seawater, urine, floodwater, standing water in a collapse void, water from a well that tastes salty, water near a factory outfall, or any water with an oily sheen or an unnatural colour or smell.

## Part 3: Making Water Safe

**Always filter before you treat.** Pour through several folded layers of clean cotton cloth — a folded saree is a genuinely effective pre-filter and removes sediment, larvae, and copepods. Sediment protects pathogens from chlorine and blocks UV.

**Method 1 — Boiling (most reliable).**
- Bring to a **rolling boil for 1 minute** (3 minutes above 2,000 m — Himachal, Uttarakhand, Ladakh, Sikkim).
- Kills all bacteria, viruses, and parasites. Does not remove chemicals, salt, or heavy metals.
- Let it cool covered. Pour between two vessels to restore taste.

**Method 2 — Chlorine tablets.**
- Aquatabs, halazone, or the tablets distributed by health departments. Follow the packet — typically 1 tablet per litre, wait 30 minutes.
- Does not kill Cryptosporidium. Combine with filtration.

**Method 3 — Household bleach (sodium hypochlorite).**
- Use **plain, unscented** bleach only — no detergent, no fragrance, no colour-safe additives.
- Roughly **2 drops per litre of clear water, 4 drops per litre of cloudy water**. Stir and wait 30 minutes.
- **It should smell faintly of chlorine afterwards.** If not, repeat the dose and wait another 15 minutes.

**Method 4 — SODIS (solar disinfection).** Excellent in Indian sunlight and costs nothing.
- Fill a **clear** PET bottle (not more than 2-3 litres, not glass, not coloured) with **clear** water, no more than 10 cm deep when lying down.
- Lay it horizontally on a **dark or metal surface** — a tin roof is ideal — in full sun.
- **6 hours of strong sun, or 2 consecutive days if cloudy.**
- Does not work on cloudy water. Filter first.

**Method 5 — Ceramic candle filters, RO, and household purifiers.**
- Most Indian homes have one. **Candle filters and RO membranes remove bacteria and protozoa but many do not remove viruses,** and RO needs electricity and pressure.
- **Boil filtered water too** during an outbreak.

**What treatment does NOT fix:** salinity, pesticide, heavy metals (fluoride, arsenic — endemic in parts of West Bengal, Bihar, Assam, Punjab, and Rajasthan), and industrial chemicals. Boiling actually **concentrates** these. If the water is chemically contaminated, find another source.

## Part 4: Storing Water

- **Store in clean, food-grade containers with lids.** Used cooking-oil tins and jerry cans, well washed, are fine. Do not use containers that held chemicals or pesticide.
- **Keep treated and untreated water clearly separated**, and mark them.
- **Never dip hands or a cup into a storage container.** Pour, or use a dedicated ladle with a long handle. Hands are the main route of recontamination in a relief setting.
- **Store in the dark and the cool.** Sunlight grows algae.
- **Re-treat stored water every 2-3 days** in Indian heat, or boil before drinking.

## Part 5: Food

**Priority order for eating what you have:**
1. Perishables from the fridge, in the first 4-6 hours after power fails
2. Freezer contents next — a full freezer stays cold for about 48 hours if unopened, half full for about 24
3. Cupboard staples and dry goods
4. Sealed packets and cans last

**Throw away without tasting:**
- Anything that touched floodwater, **including sealed packets** (seals are not waterproof) and anything with a screw cap or a crimped top
- Cooked food left at Indian room temperature for more than 2 hours
- Anything with mould, a bad smell, a slimy texture, or an off colour
- Cans that are swollen, leaking, badly dented at the seam, or rusted through
- Any milk, curd, paneer, or meat that has been unrefrigerated

**Cans that are intact** can be salvaged from floodwater: remove the label, wash in soapy water, disinfect in a chlorine solution, dry, and relabel with a marker.

**Good emergency foods for an Indian household — no cooking, long shelf life, high energy:**
- Roasted chana, sattu, murmura, chuda/poha, dry fruit, peanuts
- Gur (jaggery), glucose powder, honey
- Biscuits, rusk, khakhra, thepla (lasts several days)
- Ready-to-eat packets, canned dal and vegetables
- **ORS sachets — the highest-value item per gram in any Indian emergency kit**
- Milk powder, and for infants, formula if already in use

**Rationing:** eat less but eat something every day. **Do not eat if you have no water** — digestion consumes water and dry food worsens dehydration. Water first, always.

**Cooking without gas:**
- A three-stone fire, a chulha, or a rocket stove made from a tin can
- **Never cook with charcoal, coal, or a kerosene stove inside a closed room.** Carbon monoxide is invisible, odourless, and kills whole families in their sleep. Ventilate on two sides at minimum.
- A solar cooker or a black pot in strong sun will pasteurise water and cook dal slowly

## Part 6: Sanitation — This Is What Kills

The single most important thing you can do to prevent an outbreak is to keep faeces away from water, food, and hands.

**Setting up a latrine when there is none:**
- **At least 30 metres from any water source, and downhill from it.** This is the number that matters.
- A **trench latrine**: dig a trench 30 cm wide, 60-90 cm deep, as long as needed. Keep the excavated soil beside it and **cover each use with soil or ash**.
- **Cat-hole** for one person: 20 cm deep, covered after each use, at least 30 m from water.
- **Bucket toilet with a lid** for a house or a shelter: line it, add ash or lime after each use, and empty into a deep pit far from water and habitation.
- **Mark the latrine area clearly** and keep it away from the cooking and sleeping area.
- **Provide a handwashing point at the exit** — even a bottle with a hole in the cap hanging from a string, and a bar of soap on a string.

**Handwashing is the single highest-impact intervention in a relief setting.**
- With soap, for 20 seconds, before eating, before cooking, after the toilet, after handling waste, after handling a body, after touching a sick person
- **A "tippy tap"** — a hanging bottle or can with a small hole, tipped by a foot lever — uses a fraction of the water of a tap and works in a camp
- If you have no soap: ash and sand rubbed on wet hands is genuinely effective. Plain water is much better than nothing.

**Menstrual hygiene** is routinely neglected in Indian relief settings and causes real harm. Ensure a private space, water, soap, cloth or pads, and a disposal point. Cloth must be washed in clean water, dried in **direct sunlight** (not hidden in the dark, which causes infection), and stored dry.

**Waste and bodies:**
- Bury or burn organic waste. Keep it away from the water source and downwind.
- **Dead bodies do not cause epidemics** — this is a persistent and harmful myth that has led to mass burials without identification in Indian disasters. Handle them with dignity, use gloves, wash hands, **photograph and record identifying details before burial**, and follow the district administration's instructions. The living, not the dead, are the source of disease.
- **Dead animals** are a genuine hazard and should be buried deep, away from water, or disposed of as directed.

## Part 7: Diarrhoea and ORS

**ORS is the treatment. Fluid, not medicine, is what saves lives.**

**Making ORS correctly:**
- **One full WHO-formula sachet in exactly 1 litre of clean water.** Not more concentrated — a stronger solution is dangerous, especially for children.
- **Homemade if you have no sachet:** **6 level teaspoons of sugar + half a level teaspoon of salt in 1 litre of clean water.** Stir until dissolved.
- **Taste it. It should be no saltier than tears.** If it is saltier, throw it away and remake it.
- Discard after 24 hours and make fresh.

**Giving it:**
- **Small sips, continuously.** A spoonful every 1-2 minutes for a child.
- **Keep giving it even if they are vomiting.** Wait 10 minutes after a vomit and restart with smaller, slower sips. Some gets absorbed.
- **After every loose stool:** a child under 2 needs about a quarter to half a cup; an older child about half to one cup; an adult as much as they will take.
- **Keep breastfeeding an infant.** Do not stop. Breast milk is the best fluid an infant can have.
- **Keep feeding.** Withholding food prolongs diarrhoea and causes malnutrition. Give khichdi, curd, banana, rice water (kanji), dal water.
- **Zinc supplements for 14 days** shorten childhood diarrhoea significantly and are available free at government facilities.

**Danger signs — get to a hospital:**
- Blood in the stool
- Repeated vomiting so nothing stays down
- No urine for 8 hours (adult) or 6 hours (child)
- Sunken eyes, skin that stays tented when pinched, a sunken fontanelle in an infant
- Lethargy, floppiness, or difficulty waking
- Sunken eyes with cold, mottled hands and feet
- High fever
- **Rice-water stools in huge volume with rapid collapse — this is cholera and it needs IV fluids and antibiotics urgently**

**Do not give anti-diarrhoeal drugs (loperamide) to children.** They trap the infection and cause serious harm.

## Part 8: Disease to Expect After an Indian Disaster

| Disease | Onset | Key sign | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acute watery diarrhoea / cholera | 1-5 days | Rice-water stool, rapid dehydration | ORS immediately, hospital for severe |
| Typhoid | 1-3 weeks | Stepwise rising fever, abdominal pain | Doctor, antibiotics |
| Hepatitis A and E | 2-8 weeks | Jaundice, dark urine, nausea. **Hepatitis E is very dangerous in pregnancy** | Rest, fluids, doctor |
| Leptospirosis | 2-30 days | Fever, severe calf pain, red eyes, jaundice, after wading in floodwater | **Doctor urgently — treatable, lethal if missed** |
| Dengue | 4-10 days | High fever, severe body ache, rash, low platelets | Fluids, paracetamol only. **No aspirin or ibuprofen.** Watch for warning signs |
| Malaria | 1-4 weeks | Fever with chills, often cyclical | Test and treat |
| Scrub typhus | 1-3 weeks | Fever, an eschar (black scab) at a bite site | Doctor — doxycycline works |
| Measles | 1-2 weeks | Fever, rash, in crowded camps | Vaccinate children in camps |
| Skin and fungal infection | Days | Rashes, itching between toes | Keep dry, antifungal |
| Respiratory infection | Days | Cough, fever, in crowded shelters | Ventilate, separate the sick |

**Dengue warning signs that mean go to hospital now:** severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, bleeding from gums or nose, black stools, restlessness or lethargy, cold clammy skin, and a sudden drop in temperature with worsening condition.

## Part 9: Camp and Shelter Hygiene

- **Ventilate.** Respiratory infection and TB spread fast in crowded, closed shelters.
- **Separate the sick**, especially anyone with diarrhoea or a cough, and give them a dedicated toilet or bucket if possible.
- **One food handler, with clean hands, serving everyone** is safer than everyone serving themselves.
- **Cook thoroughly and eat immediately.** Do not store cooked food in Indian heat.
- **Cover all food and water.** Flies are the main vector.
- **Empty all standing water weekly** — coolers, pots, tyres, tarpaulin folds — to stop mosquito breeding.
- **Vaccinate children** — measles and the routine schedule — as soon as health teams arrive. Camps are where outbreaks start.
- **Report clusters of illness immediately** to the health worker, ANM, or ASHA. Early reporting is what stops an outbreak.

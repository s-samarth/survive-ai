# Cyclones, Storm Surge and Tsunami (India)

India has a 7,500 km coastline and is hit by roughly 5-6 tropical cyclones a year. The Bay of Bengal is far more dangerous than the Arabian Sea and produces the deadliest storm surges on Earth. Odisha, Andhra Pradesh, West Bengal, and Tamil Nadu are the highest-risk states; Gujarat and Maharashtra face increasing Arabian Sea activity.

**Most cyclone deaths in India are from storm surge and freshwater flooding, not wind.** The surge is a wall of seawater pushed ashore by the storm, and it can be several metres high and reach kilometres inland over flat coastal terrain.

---

## Part 1: Reading the Warning

IMD issues a four-stage warning for the coast. Learn the timings — they tell you how much time you have.

| Stage | Lead time | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Pre-Cyclone Watch** | 72 hours | A system is developing. Start preparing. |
| **Cyclone Alert** | 48 hours | Expected to affect the coast. Secure everything, plan evacuation. |
| **Cyclone Warning** | 24 hours | Landfall expected. Evacuate if told to. |
| **Post-Landfall Outlook** | 12 hours | Landfall imminent. Be in shelter now. |

**IMD colour codes**: green = no action; yellow = be aware; orange = be prepared; red = take action. A red alert for your district means take action now, not later.

**Categories by wind speed** (IMD scale): Cyclonic Storm 62-88 kmph; Severe 89-117; Very Severe 118-166; Extremely Severe 167-221; Super Cyclonic Storm above 222 kmph.

**Physical warning signs if you have no forecast:** a long, heavy swell arriving on a calm day; a rapid, sustained drop in air pressure; an unusually red sky at sunrise and sunset; high thin cirrus cloud streaming in from one direction; increasing wind with squally rain; birds leaving the coast inland; the sea level rising unusually at high tide.

## Part 2: 48 to 72 Hours Out

1. **Find out where your cyclone shelter is.** Odisha and Andhra Pradesh have thousands of Multi-Purpose Cyclone Shelters (MPCS) built after the 1999 super cyclone. Know the route on foot. This preparation is the single biggest reason Odisha's death tolls collapsed from over 10,000 in 1999 to double digits in later storms.
2. **Trim or remove overhanging branches and coconut fronds.** A falling coconut palm is a lethal projectile.
3. **Secure or bring inside everything loose**: GI sheets, hoardings, flower pots, cycles, water drums, tin roofing, cattle feed. Anything unsecured becomes a missile at 150 kmph.
4. **Strengthen the roof.** In kutcha and tin-roof houses, tie the roof down with rope over the top, anchored to the ground or heavy weights on both sides. Cyclone deaths in tin-roofed houses are usually from the roof lifting off.
5. **Board or tape windows.** Tape does not stop glass breaking but holds shards together. Plywood or planks nailed across is far better.
6. **Store water**: 3 litres per person per day for at least 5 days. Fill every drum, bucket, and bottle.
7. **Store dry food that needs no cooking.** Chuda/poha, murmura, chana, gur, biscuits, dry fruits.
8. **Charge everything.** Buy candles, matches, kerosene, batteries.
9. **Documents and cash** in double plastic bags.
10. **Move livestock inland or release them.** Tethered animals drown. In coastal Odisha and Andhra, releasing cattle before the surge saves them.

## Part 3: Evacuation

**If a coastal evacuation is ordered, go. Immediately.**

- Storm surge is not survivable in a ground-floor house near the coast. There is no version of "riding it out" that works.
- **Go inland and uphill, not just inland.** Even 5 metres of elevation matters enormously.
- Take: water, dry food, ORS, medicines, documents, cash, torch, powerbank, a change of clothes, a rope, a whistle, blankets, and identification for every person.
- Carry a **stick** — for probing water and for snakes.
- Do not travel once the wind is above roughly 60 kmph. Flying debris and falling trees make roads lethal. If you left it too late, shelter in the strongest building available.
- Leave a note on the door with names, number of people, destination, date and time.

## Part 4: During the Cyclone

**Stay indoors, in the strongest room, away from all windows.**

- The safest place is a small interior room on the ground floor of a pucca (masonry/RCC) building — a bathroom or a stairwell — **unless surge or flooding is expected**, in which case go up.
- **Get under a sturdy table** and cover yourself with a mattress or blanket against flying glass.
- **Do not open windows to "equalise pressure".** This is a myth. It lets wind in, and internal pressure lifts the roof off.
- **Do not go outside during the eye.** The calm in the middle lasts 20-60 minutes and is followed by wind from the exact opposite direction, at full strength, hitting structures already weakened from the other side. Every Indian cyclone kills people who went outside during the eye. **Wait until the wind has been steady from the new direction for at least an hour.**
- **Do not use candles if there is any chance of a gas leak.** Torches only.
- **Turn off electricity at the main** and the gas at the regulator.
- **Fill the bathtub and buckets** before the supply fails.
- **Keep a radio on** — All India Radio broadcasts through cyclones and is the most reliable information source when networks fail.
- **Listen for the roof.** If the roof starts to go, move to the room furthest downwind, get under solid furniture, and cover everyone with mattresses.

## Part 5: Storm Surge

The surge is the killer. Understand it.

- It arrives around landfall, usually on the right side of the track in the northern hemisphere, and coincides catastrophically with high tide.
- Bay of Bengal surges have exceeded 5 metres. In the flat deltaic terrain of the Sundarbans, coastal Odisha, and the Godavari-Krishna delta, water travels many kilometres inland.
- It comes fast — the water rises in minutes, not hours — and it carries debris, boats, and vehicles.
- **There is no swimming in a storm surge.** Elevation is the only defence.
- **If you are caught:** get to the highest solid structure available — an upper floor, a concrete roof, a shelter, an embankment, a large tree as an absolute last resort. Tie yourself and children to something solid with cloth or rope so you are not swept away if you lose consciousness.

## Part 6: Tsunami

Different from a cyclone: no warning wind, no rain, and it comes after an earthquake.

**At risk in India:** the entire east coast (Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Odisha, West Bengal, Puducherry), Kerala's coast, and above all the **Andaman and Nicobar Islands**, which have almost no warning time because the source is right there.

**Natural warning signs — act on any one of these without waiting for an official warning:**
1. **A strong earthquake at the coast**, or one that makes it hard to stand, or a weaker one that lasts unusually long
2. **The sea suddenly withdrawing**, exposing reef and seabed. This is the trough of the wave arriving first. People walked out to collect fish in 2004 and were killed.
3. **A loud roaring sound from the sea**, like a train or aircraft
4. **An unusual, rapid rise in sea level** with no storm

**Action:**
- **Run inland and uphill immediately.** On foot is often faster than by vehicle — roads jam.
- Target **at least 30 metres of elevation, or 3 km inland**, whichever you reach first.
- If neither is reachable, get to the **third floor or higher of a strong RCC building**, or climb a strong tree.
- **The first wave is usually not the biggest.** Waves arrive for hours. **Stay high for at least 8-12 hours** or until officials declare it over.
- **Never go to the beach to watch.** If you can see the wave, you cannot outrun it.
- India's **INCOIS** tsunami warning centre in Hyderabad issues alerts, but for a near-field source in the Andaman arc the wave may arrive before any message does. Trust the natural signs.

## Part 7: After the Storm

- **Wait for the official all-clear**, not just for the wind to stop.
- **Assume every fallen wire is live.** Assume every puddle touching a wire is live.
- **Watch for weakened trees and hanging branches** — they fall for days afterwards.
- **Snakes and scorpions** are displaced and will be in houses, debris, and under anything you lift.
- **Do not drink tap water** until authorities confirm it. Boil or chlorinate. Coastal wells are contaminated with seawater and sewage after a surge.
- **Salt contamination:** after a surge, agricultural land and wells may be saline for seasons. Do not drink from a well that tastes salty — it will worsen dehydration.
- **Beware collapsing structures** weakened by wind and water.
- **Register at the relief camp** and with the local revenue official for compensation.
- **Watch for the disease wave** — diarrhoea, cholera, leptospirosis, dengue. See the **Water, Food and Sanitation** guide.

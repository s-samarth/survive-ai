# Snakebite, Animal Bites and Stings (India)

India has the highest snakebite mortality in the world — roughly 58,000 deaths a year, overwhelmingly in rural areas, overwhelmingly at night, and overwhelmingly among farmers and their families. Almost all of these are preventable, and almost all of them involve delay caused by traditional first aid that does not work.

**The one thing that saves lives is anti-snake venom (ASV) in a hospital. Everything in the first aid section exists only to get the person there alive and unharmed by well-meaning treatment.**

---

## Part 1: The Big Four

Indian polyvalent ASV is manufactured against four species, which cause the large majority of deaths:

**1. Spectacled Cobra (Naja naja / nag)**
- Hood with spectacle mark, 1-2 m, found across India
- **Neurotoxic.** Drooping eyelids, double vision, difficulty swallowing and speaking, then paralysis of the breathing muscles
- Local swelling and tissue death at the bite site is common

**2. Common Krait (Bungarus caeruleus / karait)**
- Slim, glossy blue-black with paired white bands, up to 1.7 m
- **The most dangerous snake in India for one reason: the bite is often painless and the person goes to sleep.** Kraits enter houses at night and bite people sleeping on the floor. The victim wakes with abdominal pain, or does not wake at all.
- **Neurotoxic**, with very little local swelling — so the bite is dismissed as an insect bite
- **Any person who wakes at night with unexplained severe abdominal pain, difficulty opening their eyes, or difficulty swallowing, in a krait area, must be taken to hospital immediately as a suspected krait bite.**

**3. Russell's Viper (Daboia russelii)**
- Heavy-bodied, brown with three rows of dark oval spots ringed in black and white, loud hiss
- **Haemotoxic.** Severe pain and swelling, bleeding from gums and injection sites, blood in urine, and **kidney failure**
- The commonest cause of snakebite death in many Indian states

**4. Saw-scaled Viper (Echis carinatus)**
- Small, under 60 cm, rough keeled scales it rubs together to make a sizzling sound
- **Haemotoxic**, causes persistent bleeding and non-clotting blood
- Very common in arid and semi-arid India

**Also dangerous but not covered well by standard polyvalent ASV:** the hump-nosed pit viper (Western Ghats, Sri Lanka region) and the king cobra. Hospitals in those regions know this.

## Part 2: Snakebite First Aid — What To Do

**The mnemonic is: Do It R.I.G.H.T. — Reassure, Immobilise, Get to Hospital, Tell the doctor.**

1. **Reassure the person.** Around 70% of snakebites in India are by non-venomous snakes, and of venomous bites, many are "dry bites" with no venom injected. Panic raises heart rate and speeds venom spread. Say this out loud to them: "Most snakebites are not fatal. We are going to the hospital now. Stay still."

2. **Immobilise the bitten limb** exactly as you would a fracture — with a splint and a sling. **Do not let them walk if you can avoid it.** Muscle movement pumps venom into the circulation.

3. **Keep the bite at or below heart level.**

4. **Remove rings, bangles, chudiyan, watches, anklets, and tight clothing** from the bitten limb immediately, before swelling begins. Swelling traps them and they cut off circulation.

5. **Get to a hospital that stocks ASV. Now.** Not a traditional healer, not a temple, not a local clinic without ASV. Ask specifically: "Do you have anti-snake venom?" District hospitals and most CHCs stock it; PHCs increasingly do.

6. **Note the time of the bite** and write it down.

7. **Photograph the snake from a safe distance if it is already dead or clearly visible.** **Do not try to catch or kill it** — a large share of second bites happen to people trying to capture the snake. Identification is helpful but not required; Indian polyvalent ASV covers all four.

8. **Watch the breathing.** In neurotoxic envenoming, the person dies because their breathing muscles stop, not because of the venom itself. If they stop breathing on the way, **rescue breaths or bag-mask ventilation keeps them alive until ASV works.** Many krait and cobra deaths in India are people who could have been ventilated.

9. **Mark the edge of the swelling with a pen every 15 minutes** and note the time. This tells the doctor how fast the envenoming is progressing.

## Part 3: Snakebite — What NOT To Do

Every one of these is common in India and every one of them causes harm.

- **DO NOT apply a tourniquet.** This is the single most damaging traditional practice. Tourniquets do not stop venom spread; they cause limb ischaemia, gangrene, and amputation, and when released they can dump a bolus of venom into the circulation. **If someone has already applied one, do not remove it until you are at a hospital with ASV available** — sudden release can cause collapse.
- **DO NOT cut the bite site.** It causes uncontrollable bleeding in viper bites and introduces infection.
- **DO NOT suck the venom out** — by mouth, with a syringe, or with a "snake stone". It does not work and it poisons the sucker's mouth ulcers.
- **DO NOT apply ice, chemicals, herbs, cow dung, chillies, kerosene, or a hot iron.**
- **DO NOT give alcohol, aspirin, or ibuprofen.** Aspirin and ibuprofen worsen bleeding in viper bites. Paracetamol is acceptable for pain.
- **DO NOT wash or scrub the bite site vigorously** — residual venom on the skin can help identification in some hospitals. Wipe gently.
- **DO NOT go to a traditional healer, a temple, or a "snake-stone" practitioner.** This is the leading cause of death by delay in rural India. Every hour lost is measurable mortality.
- **DO NOT wait to see if symptoms develop.** Go immediately. Krait bites in particular have a delay of hours before the person deteriorates suddenly and irreversibly.

## Part 4: Preventing Snakebite

Most Indian snakebites happen to people walking barefoot at night, or sleeping on the floor.

- **Wear closed shoes or gumboots when walking outside after dark**, especially in fields, on bunds, and after rain
- **Always carry a torch at night and use it.** Look where you put your feet and your hands.
- **Sleep on a cot, not on the floor.** This single change would prevent a very large share of Indian krait deaths.
- **Use a mosquito net tucked in under the mattress** — it is also an effective snake barrier
- **Do not put your hands into holes, under rocks, into grain heaps, woodpiles, or thatch** without looking
- **Keep the area around the house clear** of rubbish, rubble, firewood stacks, and dense vegetation — these harbour rats, and rats bring snakes
- **Store grain in sealed containers** and control rodents
- **Close gaps under doors** at night
- **Shake out shoes, clothes, and bedding** before use
- **During floods, expect snakes on any high ground, in houses, and in debris.** Check before you sit, lie down, or reach.

## Part 5: Scorpions and Spiders

**Scorpion sting** — the Indian red scorpion (Hottentotta tamulus) of Maharashtra, Gujarat, Karnataka and Andhra is genuinely dangerous, particularly to children.
- **Signs of serious envenoming:** severe local pain, sweating, vomiting, restlessness, cold clammy extremities, and then **pulmonary oedema and heart failure** in children
- **Treatment:** clean the site, cold compress for pain, paracetamol, and **get to a hospital**. Prazosin is the specific treatment for Indian red scorpion envenoming and it is highly effective; scorpion antivenom is also available.
- Do not cut, suck, or apply a tourniquet.

**Spiders** — no Indian spider is reliably lethal to a healthy adult. Clean the bite, cold compress, watch for infection and for spreading tissue damage, and see a doctor if it worsens.

**Centipedes** — painful but not dangerous. Wash, cold compress, painkiller.

## Part 6: Dog, Monkey and Animal Bites — Rabies

**India accounts for roughly a third of the world's rabies deaths.** Rabies is 100% fatal once symptoms appear and 100% preventable before they do. Dogs cause most cases; monkeys, cats, bats, mongooses, and jackals also transmit it.

**Immediately after any bite, scratch, or lick on broken skin:**

1. **Wash the wound with soap and running water for a full 15 minutes.** This is not a formality — thorough washing alone reduces rabies risk substantially. Use plenty of water and physically flush the wound.
2. **Apply an antiseptic** — povidone-iodine (Betadine) or alcohol — after washing.
3. **Do not apply chilli powder, oil, turmeric, lime, or herbs. Do not suture the wound. Do not cauterise.** These are common Indian practices and all of them increase risk.
4. **Go for post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) immediately.** The anti-rabies vaccine is **free at government hospitals across India.** Do not delay for any reason.
5. **For a deep bite, a bite on the head, neck, face, hands, or genitals, or multiple bites, you also need rabies immunoglobulin (RIG)** infiltrated into the wound. Ask for it specifically. It is available at district hospitals and medical colleges.
6. **Complete the full vaccine schedule.** Stopping halfway because the dog "looks fine" is a common and fatal error.
7. **Get a tetanus booster** if it has been over 5 years.
8. **Observe the animal if possible** for 10 days — but **start the vaccine anyway**. If the animal is alive and well after 10 days, the course can sometimes be stopped on medical advice.

**Category of exposure:**
- Touching or feeding an animal, licks on intact skin — no PEP needed
- Nibbling of uncovered skin, minor scratches without bleeding — vaccine
- Single or multiple bites or scratches that break the skin, licks on broken skin, contact with bats — **vaccine plus immunoglobulin**

**Monkey bites** are extremely common in Indian temple towns, hill stations, and cities. Treat exactly as above. Monkeys also transmit herpes B virus.

**Snake, dog, and monkey bites all need tetanus cover.**

## Part 7: Insect Stings and Anaphylaxis

**Bee, wasp and hornet stings:**
- **Remove a bee sting by scraping sideways** with a card or a fingernail. Do not squeeze it with tweezers — that injects the remaining venom.
- Wash, apply a cold compress, take an antihistamine (cetirizine) for itching
- **Multiple stings (dozens to hundreds) can cause kidney failure and are a medical emergency**, even without allergy. Indian giant honeybee (Apis dorsata) mass attacks are a real hazard near forests and old buildings.
- **If attacked by a swarm: run, in a straight line, covering your face, for at least 100 metres.** Do not swat, do not jump into water (they wait), do not stand still. Get indoors or into a vehicle.

**Anaphylaxis** — the emergency. Signs within minutes: swelling of the lips, tongue, or throat; a tight throat or hoarse voice; wheeze or difficulty breathing; widespread rash; dizziness or collapse.
- **Adrenaline into the outer thigh is the treatment** if an auto-injector is available. Through clothing is fine.
- **Lie them flat with the legs raised** unless they cannot breathe lying down, in which case sit them up. **Do not let them stand or walk** — this can cause sudden fatal collapse.
- **An antihistamine tablet is not treatment for anaphylaxis.** Get to a hospital urgently.
- Anyone who has had one severe reaction should carry an adrenaline auto-injector.

## Part 8: Leeches, Ticks and Water Hazards

**Leeches** (Western Ghats, North East, monsoon forests): do not pull, burn, or apply salt while attached — this makes them regurgitate into the wound. Slide a fingernail or a card sideways under the sucker to detach. Then clean and press — leech bites bleed for hours because of the anticoagulant, which is harmless. Cover with a dressing. Prevent with tucked-in trousers, leech socks, and tobacco or salt paste on footwear.

**Ticks:** grasp as close to the skin as possible with tweezers and pull straight out, steadily. Do not twist, burn, or smother with oil. Clean the site. Watch for fever and rash over the next 2 weeks — India has scrub typhus, Indian tick typhus, and Kyasanur Forest Disease (Western Ghats), all of which are treatable and dangerous if missed.

**Mosquitoes:** the biggest killer insect in India. Malaria, dengue, chikungunya, and Japanese encephalitis all follow floods and standing water. Use nets, cover up at dawn and dusk (dengue mosquitoes bite by day), use repellent, and **eliminate all standing water** — coolers, pots, tyres, tanks, and roof gutters — every week.

**Stonefish, sea urchins and stingrays** (Indian coasts): for stonefish and stingray, **immerse the wound in water as hot as can be tolerated for 30-90 minutes** — the venom is heat-labile and this genuinely relieves pain. Remove visible spines. Get medical care; these wounds infect badly and stingray barbs can penetrate deeply.

**Jellyfish** (common on Indian coasts, especially the west coast in monsoon): rinse with **seawater or vinegar, not fresh water** — fresh water triggers remaining stinging cells to fire. Remove tentacles with a card or gloved hand, not bare fingers. Then hot water immersion for pain. Do not urinate on it.

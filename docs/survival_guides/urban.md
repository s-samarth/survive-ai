# Urban Disaster Survival Guide

Urban emergencies — earthquakes, fires, civil unrest, infrastructure collapse, flooding — are uniquely dangerous because the city itself becomes the threat. Buildings collapse, utilities fail, and crowds become deadly. This guide covers the most critical skills for surviving in a compromised urban environment.

---

## Part 1: Immediate Response to Urban Disasters

### Earthquake

**During shaking:**
1. **Drop, Cover, Hold On**: Drop to your hands and knees immediately. Get under a sturdy desk or table and hold on. If no table, get against an interior wall and protect your head and neck with your arms
2. Stay inside during shaking — most injuries happen when people try to run outside and are struck by falling glass and debris as they exit
3. Stay away from windows, bookcases, refrigerators, and anything that could fall or shatter
4. If in bed: stay there and protect your head with a pillow
5. If in a high-rise: do not attempt to run down stairs during shaking — you will be thrown

**After shaking stops:**
1. Check yourself and others for injuries before moving
2. Expect aftershocks — they can be nearly as powerful as the main quake
3. Do not use elevators — they may be structurally compromised or power may fail
4. Before exiting, check the door frame — if it is bowed or warped, the structure may be compromised. Open carefully and brace the door in case it is all that is holding up the ceiling above you
5. Smell for gas — a rotten egg smell means a gas leak. Do not use lighters, turn on lights, or create any spark. Open windows and leave immediately
6. If you are trapped under rubble: see "Trapped Under Rubble" section below

### Building Fire

**Immediate actions:**
1. When you smell smoke or hear a fire alarm, do not ignore it — act immediately
2. **Feel the door before opening**: Use the back of your hand on the door and door handle. If hot, do not open — fire is directly on the other side. If cool, open slowly and be prepared to close immediately if smoke or heat rushes in
3. **Stay low**: Smoke and toxic gases rise — cleaner air is near the floor. Crawl if necessary
4. Take your key with you — you may need to return to your room if escape routes are blocked
5. **Close doors behind you**: A closed door can hold back fire and smoke for 30+ minutes, buying time for escape and rescue. Do not prop doors open
6. Use stairs, never elevators
7. If your exit route is blocked by smoke, return to your room, close the door, seal gaps under the door with clothing or towels, open a window for air, and signal for help

**If you are on fire:**
- Stop, Drop, and Roll — do not run, it fans the flames
- Smother flames with a jacket, blanket, or heavy clothing

**In smoke without protection:**
- Breathe through your nose (filters slightly better than mouth)
- Wet a cloth and breathe through it — this reduces irritant particle inhalation but does NOT filter carbon monoxide
- Carbon monoxide is the primary killer in fires — it is odorless, colorless, and lethal quickly. Get out before the environment is obviously smoke-filled

### Flooding

**During flash flooding:**
1. Do not attempt to walk through moving water — 6 inches of fast-moving water can knock an adult down; 2 feet can carry away a vehicle
2. Move to higher ground immediately — do not wait for official instruction if water is rising
3. Avoid storm drains, drainage channels, and river banks — these fill rapidly and catastrophically
4. If caught in a flooded vehicle: attempt to exit as water enters, before the vehicle is swept or submerged. If submerged: wait for the pressure to equalize (vehicle fills to door level), then open the door or break the side window (not the windshield), and swim to the surface. Do not panic — this process takes 30–60 seconds

**Contamination after flooding:**
- Floodwater is contaminated with sewage, chemicals, and pathogens
- Do not drink floodwater or eat food that has been submerged — even canned goods if the label has been compromised
- Wash hands and skin thoroughly after any contact with floodwater
- Boil all water or use purification tablets even from tap water, as treatment plants may have failed

## Part 2: Escape Routes and Movement

### Planning Urban Escape Routes

Before a crisis, know your environment:
1. Map three different routes out of your neighborhood to a safe destination — one primary and two alternatives in case roads are blocked, collapsed, or controlled
2. Identify which streets have the most building density (greater collapse/debris risk) vs. wide open areas
3. Know where parks, parking lots, and open spaces are — these are natural rally points away from falling structures
4. Know your city's emergency shelter locations: schools, community centers, sports stadiums
5. Identify hospitals — important both as shelter and as destinations for injured people

### Moving Through a Chaotic City

**General rules:**
- Move during daylight when you can see hazards
- Move in small groups — 2-5 people. Large groups are slow, visible, and attract attention; individuals are vulnerable
- Do not take the most obvious route — main roads will be gridlocked, targeted, or under scrutiny
- Carry only what you can move quickly with
- Keep hands visible and away from pockets when near security forces or agitated crowds

**Avoiding chokepoints:**
- Bridges are critical — they concentrate movement, they can collapse in earthquakes, and they become control points during civil unrest. Know the bridges on your route and have alternatives
- Narrow alleys can be death traps: crowd crushes, collapsed walls, and easy blockades. Choose wide streets where possible
- Underpasses and tunnels: can flood rapidly, collapse in earthquakes, and become smoke-filled fire traps — use only when the surface is clearly more dangerous
- Underground transit systems: only use if the system is operating; closed underground systems become mazes of darkness, heat, and flooding

**Reading crowd behavior:**
- A moving crowd is safer than a stationary crowd — stationary crowds indicate a blockage ahead and compression risk
- Watch the direction people are running FROM — not TO. Do not run toward the chaos
- Crowd crushes kill: if compressed in a crowd, cross your arms in front of your chest to maintain breathing space. Move sideways across the crowd flow to reach an edge
- If you fall in a crowd: get up immediately. If you cannot, roll into a fetal position protecting your head, covering the back of your neck with clasped hands, and draw your knees toward your chest

### Reading Urban Terrain for Safety

- **Structural integrity indicators**: Look up constantly. Cracks in masonry that run diagonally (not straight down) indicate structural stress. Separated or bowed walls. Visible tilt in the building. Any of these mean evacuate or don't enter
- **Height**: Higher floors are better during flooding; lower floors and interior rooms are better during bombing or high wind
- **Street level hazards**: Broken gas lines smell like rotten eggs. Downed power lines — treat the ground for 10 meters around them as electrified. Standing water near electrical infrastructure can be electrified
- **Fires**: Fire travels upward and with the wind. Position yourself upwind and downhill from fires when possible

## Part 3: Shelter in an Urban Collapse

### Choosing a Safe Shelter Location

Not all urban structures are equal. Make deliberate choices:

**Best structural shelter (in order):**
1. Reinforced concrete buildings (modern construction post-1970 in most countries) — pillars and floor slabs create survivable void spaces even when damaged
2. Basements of stone or brick buildings — below-grade position provides natural protection from collapse above
3. Interior stairwells of concrete buildings — stairwells are among the most reinforced parts of any structure
4. Parking structures — open plans with reinforced columns can survive partial collapse

**Worst locations:**
- Old unreinforced masonry (brick or stone walls without rebar) — these disintegrate in earthquakes, the masonry is lethal
- Structures with flat heavy roofs — these pancake in earthquakes and collapse catastrophically
- Any building with obvious previous damage: sagging, visible structural cracking, unusual angles
- Ground floors of high-rises — upper floors collapse onto them
- Any building near a fire — radiant heat and collapse risk expand as the fire grows

### Trapped Under Rubble

If you or someone is trapped after a collapse:

1. **Do not shout continuously** — it exhausts your air, dehydrates you, and your voice is exhausted quickly. Instead, shout in three loud bursts at regular intervals (every 5–10 minutes), then stop and listen
2. **Tap on pipes, concrete, or metal** using a hard object — tapping transmits sound much farther through structure than voice alone. Signal in patterns of three (SOS)
3. **Turn off your phone screen but keep the phone on** — rescuers use listening devices, and your phone's radio signal can be detected. Send a text if you have any signal (texts transmit on much less bandwidth than calls)
4. **Conserve energy and stay still** if you are not in immediate danger — movement disturbs unstable rubble and can cause secondary collapse
5. **Protect your breathing space**: cover your nose and mouth with clothing to filter dust. Concrete dust causes severe lung damage
6. **Create light if possible**: a lit phone screen can be seen through small gaps and helps orient rescuers
7. **If you are not injured and can move**: use caution. Test every surface before putting weight on it. Move slowly toward light or air movement (indicates an opening)

### Improvised Urban Shelter

If you have lost your home and need shelter:

**Best improvised shelter locations:**
- Ground floor of structurally intact building: interior rooms only, away from exterior walls and glass
- Community centers, schools, large commercial buildings — designed for many occupants, with emergency systems
- Multi-story car park: open structure, less collapse risk, sheltered from weather on upper levels

**Making a room safer:**
- Push heavy furniture (sofas, armchairs) to create a protected "nest" in the corner farthest from windows
- Wedge doors shut with furniture — not to lock them but to prevent them slamming open in blast pressure or wind
- If no heating: insulate the floor (cardboard, clothing, anything between you and the cold floor), minimize the space you are heating, and huddle together
- Black out windows if you do not want your location known (conflict situations) — use dark clothing, garbage bags, or blankets

## Part 4: Utilities Failure

### When Water Service Fails

**Immediate actions:**
1. Fill every available container immediately — bathtubs (150–300L), pots, bottles, buckets
2. Stop flushing the toilet unless essential — every flush uses 6–15 liters of water
3. Toilet tanks (not the bowl) hold 6–10 liters of clean water each
4. Water heaters hold 100–200 liters — turn the unit off (this prevents damage and keeps the water clean), then drain from the tap at the base
5. Building water towers or rooftop tanks may hold residual water for hours after street pressure fails

**Water purification without running utilities:**
- **Boiling**: Bring to a rolling boil for 1 minute (3 minutes at altitude above 2000m). Most effective against biological contamination
- **Chlorine tablets or bleach**: 2 drops of unscented household bleach (5–6% sodium hypochlorite) per liter of clear water, or 4 drops if turbid. Wait 30 minutes before drinking
- **Iodine tablets**: follow package instructions. Do not use for infants, pregnant women, or people with thyroid problems
- **SODIS (solar disinfection)**: fill clear plastic bottles with water, lay in direct sunlight for 6+ hours (or 2 days if overcast). Reduces biological contamination significantly but does not remove chemical contamination

**Water you can use:**
- Running tap water collected during supply (collect at first sign of problems)
- Rainwater — collect from awnings, tarps, or angled surfaces into containers
- Water from sealed, undamaged pipes (open a tap on a lower floor after shutting off the supply valve — the water in the pipes drains out clean)

### When Power Fails

1. Unplug sensitive electronics — power surges occur when supply is restored and can damage equipment
2. Keep refrigerator and freezer closed — a full freezer keeps food safe for 48+ hours if not opened; a half-full freezer for 24 hours; a refrigerator for 4 hours
3. Use flashlights — never use candles in structurally damaged buildings (fire hazard) or when gas smells are present
4. Charge all devices immediately if power is intermittent — power banks, phones, radios should stay as charged as possible
5. Solar panels and battery packs work even during grid outages — charge whatever you can during daylight

### When Gas Infrastructure is Damaged

1. If you smell gas (rotten egg odor) inside a building: do not use any switches or flames. Leave the building immediately, leave the door open, and do not re-enter
2. Turn off the main gas valve at the meter if you know its location and it is safe to access (usually outside the building) — turn the handle perpendicular to the pipe to close
3. Call the gas company emergency line from outside the building
4. Do not return until cleared by emergency services

## Part 5: Food Security in Urban Collapse

### Immediate Food Priorities

With no resupply, a typical household has 3–7 days of food, often less. Prioritize:

1. Eat perishables first — once power fails, refrigerated food lasts 4 hours, frozen food 24–48 hours (if freezer stays closed)
2. Transition to shelf-stable: canned goods, dried beans, rice, pasta, flour, oats, nuts, jerky, instant noodles
3. Do not consume food that has been submerged in floodwater unless in sealed metal cans that have been cleaned and disinfected
4. Cooking without gas or electricity: outdoor fire (only outside, never inside), camp stove (only in ventilated areas), wood stove or fireplace

### Caloric Needs Under Stress

- Sedentary sheltering: approximately 1,500–1,800 calories/day for adults
- Active (moving, working): 2,500–3,500 calories/day
- Prioritize fats and carbohydrates for energy; protein for tissue repair
- Cooking oil is extremely calorie-dense and stores well — prioritize it

### Foraging in Urban Environments

In prolonged urban collapse without resupply:
- Many parks and planted street trees have edible fruit, nuts, or berries — know what is planted in your area
- Dandelion leaves, clover, and plantain (common urban weeds) are edible and nutritious
- Do not eat wild mushrooms unless you have confirmed expertise — misidentification is fatal
- Avoid foraging near roads (lead contamination), industrial areas (chemical contamination), and areas that may have been sprayed with pesticides

## Part 6: Security and Community

### Protecting Your Shelter

Without rule of law, security becomes a personal responsibility. The goal is deterrence without conflict.

- **Do not broadcast your resources**: people who advertise food, water, and supplies become targets. Be discreet
- **Create a visible presence**: a building that appears occupied and organized is less likely to be targeted than one that appears abandoned and vulnerable
- **Community is safety**: isolated individuals are vulnerable; a cooperative group of neighbors is far safer than any individual with resources
- **Establish watch rotations**: in extended situations, continuous human presence (active watch) deters opportunistic threats
- **Pre-plan signal systems** with neighbors: a specific knock, a light in a window, or a flag that means "safe to approach"

### Community Organization

In a prolonged urban emergency, organized community response dramatically increases survival:
1. Identify who in your building or block has medical skills, mechanical skills, language skills, or physical ability
2. Pool resources: share water, food, knowledge, and equipment collectively
3. Establish a decision-making process — even in a crisis, group decisions made democratically are more durable than unilateral control
4. Assign roles: water collection, security watch, medical care, communication, fire watch
5. Maintain a log: record what decisions were made, what resources exist, and what events have occurred — this creates accountability and continuity if leadership changes

### Mental Health During Extended Urban Survival

- Maintain routine — fixed wake times, meal times, and sleep times even when meaningless by external standards
- Allow people to grieve and feel fear — suppression leads to panic and breakdown
- Give children meaningful tasks proportionate to their age — purposeful work is psychologically protective at any age
- Limit exposure to traumatic scenes when possible — send children and the most vulnerable to interior areas when difficult situations arise
- Create moments of normalcy: shared meals, stories, simple games — these are not luxuries but necessities for group cohesion

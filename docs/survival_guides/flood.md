# Floods, Flash Floods and Drowning (India)

India is the most flood-affected large country in the world. Roughly 40 million hectares — about one eighth of the land area — is flood-prone. The Brahmaputra and Barak in Assam, the Ganga and its tributaries in Bihar and Uttar Pradesh, the Kosi ("Bihar's sorrow"), the Mahanadi in Odisha, the Godavari and Krishna in the Deccan, and the Periyar and Pamba in Kerala all flood most years. Urban flooding in Mumbai, Chennai, Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Delhi, Patna, and Guwahati is now an annual event.

Most Indian flood deaths are not from a wall of water. They are from **drowning in shallow moving water, electrocution, snakebite, and disease in the weeks after.**

---

## Part 1: Before the Water Arrives

**Warning signs you can read yourself:**
- IMD **red or orange** rainfall alert for your district (red = over 204.5 mm in 24 hours; orange = 115.6-204.4 mm)
- Continuous heavy rain for more than 6 hours in a catchment upstream of you, even if it is dry where you are
- Rising, browning, faster-moving river water carrying debris
- A roaring sound from upstream in hilly areas — this is a flash flood or a debris flow and you have seconds, not minutes
- Announcements of **dam or barrage gate opening**. This is critical in India. Reservoirs release water with little notice — Hirakud, Bhakra, Mettur, Tehri, Idukki, Almatti and hundreds of smaller dams. A downstream release can raise a river metres in an hour on a clear day.
- Water backing up out of drains and manholes in a city

**What to do with a few hours:**
1. **Move upward, not outward.** Shift people, then documents, then valuables, to the highest floor or the roof. Do not wait for the water to enter.
2. **Fill every container with clean water now**, before the supply is contaminated or the pump loses power. Bathtub, buckets, drums, bottles.
3. **Charge everything.** Phones, powerbanks, torches.
4. **Switch off electricity at the main** before water reaches any socket. Do this while your feet are dry.
5. **Close the LPG regulator** and move the cylinder to a high shelf. A floating cylinder that has been submerged is dangerous.
6. **Move documents and medicines into sealed plastic** — a double-bagged polythene packet inside a steel dabba survives immersion.
7. **Move livestock and vehicles to high ground.** Do not go back for them later.
8. **Pack a go-bag**: water, ORS, dry food, torch, powerbank, medicines, documents, cash, rope, whistle, a change of dry clothes.
9. **Tell someone where you are going.** Send an SMS with your plan before the network dies.

**If evacuation is ordered, go.** Indian flood post-mortems repeatedly find that families who refused to leave possessions behind died with them.

## Part 2: When the Water Is Rising

**The single most important number: 15 centimetres.**
- **15 cm (ankle-deep) of moving water** can knock an adult off their feet
- **30 cm** floats a hatchback
- **60 cm** sweeps away an SUV or a bus
- Half of all Indian flood deaths involve a vehicle

**Never drive or walk into moving water. Turn around.** You cannot see potholes, open manholes, washed-out road, or live cable under muddy water. In Indian cities, an open manhole under floodwater is one of the most common single causes of death.

**If you must cross still or slow water on foot:**
- Only if it is below the knee and clearly not moving
- **Use a stick to probe every step** for open drains, manholes, and holes
- Wear closed shoes — glass, nails, and rebar are everywhere
- Face upstream, move sideways, keep two points of contact
- Go in a group, linked arm-in-arm with the strongest person upstream
- Untie or loosen your bag so you can shed it instantly

**If your vehicle is caught in water:**
- **Get out immediately while the water is still shallow.** Do not wait to see.
- If the doors will not open due to water pressure, **break a side window** — headrest steel prongs, a spring-loaded punch, or a hard strike at a corner of the glass. Windscreens are laminated and will not break.
- If you cannot get out and water is filling the car, wait until the pressure equalises — when water is near the roof, the door will open. Take a breath and push.
- Never stay in a car in flowing water hoping to be rescued.

**If you are swept away:**
- **Feet downstream, on your back, head up.** This lets your legs absorb impacts instead of your head.
- Do not try to stand up in moving water more than knee-deep — foot entrapment between rocks with water pushing you over is a common drowning mechanism.
- Angle towards the bank and grab anything — a tree, a pole, a wall.
- **Do not swim against the current.** Swim across it.

## Part 3: Trapped in a Building

**Go up, but do not get trapped in an attic or a loft with no exit to the roof.** People drown in sealed lofts.

- **Get to the roof** if the water keeps rising. Take water, a torch, a whistle, bright cloth, a rope, and your phone.
- **Signal:** bright cloth tied to something high, a torch at night, a whistle in threes, a mirror flash to helicopters. Write **SOS** or **HELP** on the roof with anything that contrasts.
- **Sunlight on a roof in India will kill you before the water does.** Rig shade — a sheet, a tarpaulin, a saree over anything upright. Cover your head. Ration water.
- **Do not enter floodwater to reach a neighbour** unless you are roped and someone is anchoring you.
- **Do not use lifts.** Do not use a generator or a stove indoors.
- **Watch for snakes.** Kraits, cobras, and vipers move to high ground in a flood, and that is where you are. Check bedding, bags, corners, and under things before putting a hand or foot anywhere. See the **Bites and Stings** guide.

## Part 4: Flash Floods and Cloudbursts

Flash floods in hill areas — Uttarakhand, Himachal, the North East, the Western Ghats — kill within minutes and are the deadliest Indian flood type.

- **Never camp, park, or sleep in a dry riverbed, nala, or narrow gorge.** These fill without warning from rain kilometres upstream that you cannot see or hear.
- **If you hear a roaring or grinding sound, or the river suddenly goes muddy and starts carrying trees, move perpendicular to the channel and uphill immediately.** Do not stop for belongings.
- **Get at least 30 vertical metres above the channel**, not just further along it.
- **Glacial lake outburst floods (GLOFs)** and landslide-dammed lake bursts in the Himalaya produce a debris wave, not clean water. It moves faster than you can run and it carries boulders. Height is the only defence.
- Bridges and culverts are the first things to go. Do not shelter on or under one.

## Part 5: Drowning Rescue

**Reach, Throw, Row, Go — in that order. Never go in first.**

1. **Reach** with a stick, a bamboo, a branch, a dupatta, a rope. Lie flat on the bank so you are not pulled in.
2. **Throw** anything that floats: an empty sealed plastic can or bottle, a jerry can, a tyre tube, a thermocol sheet, a plastic drum. Two sealed 2-litre bottles in a cloth bag is a workable improvised float.
3. **Row** — use a boat, a raft, an inflated tube.
4. **Go** into the water only as a last resort, only if you are a strong swimmer, and only with a float between you and them. A panicking drowning person will climb you and drown you both.

**Once out of the water:**
- If not breathing: **5 rescue breaths first, then CPR.** Drowning casualties are oxygen-starved, so breaths matter more than in a cardiac arrest.
- **Do not try to push water out of the lungs or hold them upside down.** This wastes time and causes vomiting and aspiration.
- **Keep them warm** — hypothermia follows even in warm Indian water.
- **Take them to hospital even if they seem fine.** Water in the lungs can cause fatal deterioration up to 24 hours later.

## Part 6: Water and Disease After a Flood

This is what actually kills most people, in the weeks after the water goes down.

**Assume all flood water is sewage.** It contains latrine overflow, dead animals, industrial runoff, pesticides, and hospital waste.

**Water treatment, in order of preference:**
1. **Boil** — a rolling boil for 1 minute (3 minutes above 2000 m altitude). This is the most reliable method available in an Indian home.
2. **Chlorine tablets** — Aquatabs, halazone, or the chlorine tablets distributed by health departments. Follow the packet; usually one tablet per litre, wait 30 minutes.
3. **Bleach** — plain unscented household bleach (sodium hypochlorite). Roughly 2 drops per litre of clear water, 4 drops if cloudy. Wait 30 minutes. It must smell faintly of chlorine afterwards; if not, repeat.
4. **SODIS (solar disinfection)** — fill a clear PET bottle with clear water, lay it on a metal or dark surface in full sun for 6 hours (2 days if cloudy). Works well in Indian sunlight. Does not work for cloudy water.
5. **Filter first, always.** Pour water through several layers of clean cotton cloth to remove sediment before any treatment. A folded saree removes a surprising amount, including the copepods that carry guinea worm.

**Never assume packaged is safe** — check the seal on bottled water sold during a flood; refilling is common.

**Diseases to expect after an Indian flood:**
- **Cholera and acute diarrhoea** — within days. **ORS is the treatment.** Make it correctly: one full sachet in exactly 1 litre of clean water. If you have no sachet: 6 level teaspoons of sugar plus half a level teaspoon of salt in 1 litre. It should taste no saltier than tears. Give small sips continuously. Never stop giving fluids because the person is vomiting — keep giving small amounts.
- **Typhoid** — sustained high fever a week or two later. Needs antibiotics.
- **Leptospirosis** — from water contaminated with rat urine, entering through cuts and broken skin. Very common in Kerala, Mumbai, and Gujarat after floods. Fever, severe muscle pain especially in the calves, red eyes, jaundice. **This is treatable and lethal if missed** — get to a doctor if you have waded in floodwater and develop a fever within 2 weeks.
- **Dengue, malaria, chikungunya, and Japanese encephalitis** — from mosquitoes breeding in standing water 1-3 weeks later. Empty all standing water, use nets, cover up at dawn and dusk.
- **Skin and wound infections, and fungal infections** from prolonged immersion. Dry your feet, change socks, treat every cut.
- **Tetanus** from wounds in muddy water.

**Food:** discard anything that touched floodwater, including sealed packets (seals fail) and anything in a fridge that lost power for over 4 hours. Sealed cans that are not dented, swollen, or rusted can be washed in chlorinated water and used.

## Part 7: Returning Home

- **Do not enter until the electricity is confirmed off** and any gas has been checked.
- **Wear rubber boots and gloves.** Snakes, glass, rusted metal, and contaminated silt.
- **Check for structural damage** before entering — undermined foundations, cracked walls, sagging ceilings. Water-soaked mud walls collapse when they dry unevenly.
- **Photograph everything before you clean** — for insurance and for state relief claims.
- **Ventilate and dry aggressively.** Mould takes hold within 48 hours in Indian humidity and causes long-term respiratory problems, especially in children and asthmatics.
- **Do not restart submerged electrical appliances or a submerged motor** until an electrician checks them.
- **Clean all surfaces with a bleach solution.** Discard mattresses, pillows, and soft furnishings that were submerged in sewage-contaminated water — they cannot be decontaminated.
- **Get a tetanus shot** if you have wounds and are not up to date.

**Relief and compensation:** register the loss with the local revenue official (patwari/talathi/village accountant) and the tehsildar. Photographs, the ration card, and land records support the claim. State Disaster Response Fund (SDRF) norms cover house damage, crop loss, livestock loss, and ex-gratia for deaths.

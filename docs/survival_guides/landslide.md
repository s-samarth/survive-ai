# Landslides, Cloudbursts and Mountain Hazards (India)

The Himalayan states — Uttarakhand, Himachal Pradesh, Jammu and Kashmir, Sikkim, and the North East — plus the Western Ghats in Kerala, Karnataka, Maharashtra and Tamil Nadu, and the Nilgiris, are all high landslide risk. Roughly 12.6% of India's land area is landslide-prone.

Landslides are the disaster most likely to cut you off completely. Roads and phone lines go together, valleys become isolated for days, and the terrain that traps you also stops helicopters flying.

---

## Part 1: Warning Signs — Hours to Days Before

Landslides almost always signal before they move. Most Indian landslide deaths are people who slept through the signs.

**In and around a building:**
- New cracks in plaster, floors, or the foundation, especially ones that widen over hours
- Doors and windows jamming for the first time
- The floor or a wall separating from the rest of the structure
- Outside walls, steps, or a retaining wall tilting or moving away from the building
- Fences, poles, trees, or a compound wall visibly leaning downhill

**On the slope:**
- New springs or seepage appearing where the ground was dry
- A stream or spring that was flowing suddenly running dry (the ground above has shifted and blocked it)
- Water in a stream turning suddenly muddy or carrying debris
- Bulging ground at the base of a slope
- New cracks or scarps appearing on the hillside
- Trees leaning or cracking; the sound of trees cracking or boulders knocking

**Sound:**
- A faint rumbling that grows louder — this means it has already started. **Move immediately.**

**Rainfall triggers:** most Indian landslides happen after **prolonged rain that has saturated the slope, followed by an intense burst**. If it has rained heavily for two or three days and then rains very hard, the risk is at its peak — often at night. Cloudbursts (over 100 mm in an hour) in Uttarakhand and Himachal produce debris flows within minutes.

## Part 2: If a Landslide Is Happening

- **Move sideways, out of the path, not downhill.** A debris flow travels far faster than you can run. Perpendicular escape is the only escape.
- **Get uphill of the flow** if there is a ridge or a rise you can reach.
- **Get behind a large, solidly rooted obstacle** — a big rock outcrop or a mature tree with a deep root system — if you cannot escape the path. A wall or a young tree will not hold.
- **Curl into a tight ball and protect your head** if you are caught. Keep your arms over your head and your knees to your chest.
- **If buried, try to create an air space in front of your face** with your hands and forearms as the debris settles, and try to push a hand upward so it is visible.
- **In a vehicle:** do not stop under a cut slope. If rocks are falling, drive out of the zone if the road ahead is clear; if it is not, abandon the vehicle and move sideways on foot. A car is not protection from a boulder.

## Part 3: If You Are Cut Off

This is the normal outcome in Himalayan and Western Ghats landslides. Roads take days to weeks to clear.

- **Do not attempt to walk out over fresh landslide debris.** It is unconsolidated, it moves under load, and secondary slides follow the first one for days. Every Indian monsoon produces deaths of people crossing a fresh slide on foot.
- **Do not cross below an unstable slope**, even one that has already slid. The scar above it is now unsupported.
- **Stay put and make yourself findable.** Signal fires (three fires in a triangle), bright cloth on a roof, mirror flashes towards any aircraft, a whistle in threes.
- **Ration food, not water.** Himalayan valleys usually have water; boil it, as landslide-disturbed water carries silt and contamination.
- **Consolidate the village.** Move to the strongest, safest-sited building, pool food, and share warmth. Isolation and cold kill more than the slide does after the first day.
- **Expect helicopter evacuation to be weather-dependent.** In monsoon, helicopters may not fly for days. Plan for a week, not a day.
- **Watch for landslide-dammed lakes.** A slide that blocks a river creates a lake that will burst. If a river below you suddenly stops flowing, **get high and get away from the channel immediately** — the dam is temporary and its failure produces a flash flood far worse than the original slide.

## Part 4: Where Not to Be

If you live in or travel through the Indian hills, these choices matter more than any technique.

**Do not build or sleep:**
- At the toe of a steep slope, or directly below a cut hillside
- In the mouth or channel of a nala (seasonal stream), however dry it looks
- On or just below a fresh road cut — road construction is the single biggest human cause of Himalayan slides
- On a slope where the natural drainage has been blocked by construction
- Immediately below a hillside where trees have been cleared or terracing has been abandoned

**Travel rules in the hills during monsoon:**
- **Do not travel at night.** You cannot see a slide or a washed-out road.
- **Check road status before starting.** BRO and state PWD control rooms, and local police, know which stretches are closed.
- **Do not stop in a known slide zone** to take photographs — these stretches are marked with warning boards for a reason.
- **Carry water, food, warm clothing, and a full fuel tank.** A 3-hour journey becomes a 3-day wait routinely.
- **Never try to drive across a stream flowing over a road** in the hills. The road under it may be gone.

## Part 5: Avalanches (Higher Himalaya)

Relevant in Jammu and Kashmir, Ladakh, Himachal, and Uttarakhand at altitude, and to anyone on high passes or trekking routes in winter and spring.

- **Highest risk is during and for 24 hours after heavy snowfall**, on slopes of 30-45 degrees, and on lee slopes where wind has loaded snow.
- **If caught:** shed your pack, try to move sideways off the moving slab, and "swim" hard towards the surface.
- **As it stops:** thrust one arm upward and use the other to make an air pocket in front of your face. Take a deep breath and expand your chest before the snow sets — set avalanche debris is like concrete.
- **Do not shout** unless you hear rescuers directly above you; it wastes oxygen in the air pocket.
- **Rescue from the surface:** survival drops sharply after 15 minutes. Search where the victim was last seen, downslope, probing with poles or sticks. Dig from the downhill side towards the person.
- Do not travel alone on avalanche terrain. Cross exposed slopes one person at a time with the others watching.

## Part 6: General Mountain Survival

**Altitude sickness (AMS)** — relevant on Ladakh, Spiti, Sikkim, and Himalayan trekking routes above about 2,500 m.
- Symptoms: headache, nausea, loss of appetite, fatigue, poor sleep, dizziness.
- **The treatment is descent.** Do not ascend further with symptoms.
- Danger signs — confusion, loss of coordination (cannot walk a straight line), breathlessness at rest, a wet cough or frothy sputum — mean **HACE or HAPE**, which kill within hours. **Descend immediately, at least 500-1000 m, even at night.**
- Do not ignore a headache at altitude. Do not give sedatives or sleeping tablets to someone at altitude.

**Cold at altitude:** night temperatures on a Himalayan road drop far below what daytime suggests. Wet cotton clothing at 5 C kills faster than dry clothing at -5 C. See the **Heat and Cold** guide.

**Navigation:** valley walls block GPS and phone signal. Download offline maps before you go. Follow water downhill to find habitation — Himalayan settlements are on watercourses. But do not follow a gorge you cannot climb out of.

---

## Part 7: After a Landslide

- **Stay away from the slide area.** Secondary slides are extremely common in the following days.
- **Check for injured and trapped people from a distance**, and mark locations for rescue teams rather than digging into unstable debris.
- **Watch for broken gas and electrical lines**, and for water pipes that are now contaminating the supply.
- **Report blocked rivers** to authorities as an urgent priority — a landslide dam threatens everyone downstream.
- **Replant and stabilise.** Bare slopes slide again. Vegetation, proper drainage, and retaining structures are the only long-term fix.
